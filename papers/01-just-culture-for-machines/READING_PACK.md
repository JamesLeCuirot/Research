# Reading pack: the safety-science grounding of "Just Culture for Machines"

Prepared 4 November 2026 for James, before he revises the *Safety Science* version (`submissions/safety-science/paper.tex`). This is literature preparation only. It contains no text for the paper.

## What this pack is for

An independent review found four weaknesses in the paper for *Safety Science* readers:

- it cites Reason (1997) and Marx (2001) only through the GAIN report and PSNet;
- none of its 51 references is a safety-science journal article;
- it does not engage with critiques of just culture or with empirical research on reporting and under-reporting;
- its payoff for aviation practice is two sentences.

The 29 entries below address those gaps. They include five *Safety Science* articles: four were read in abstract and one through another paper's full-text summary. Two more *Safety Science* articles are listed as found but not yet read. The pack ends with ten suggested revisions, each tied to sources in the pack. Elsevier's policy allows "Summarizing research literature in preparation for manuscript writing", but the reading and rewriting must be your own.

### 1.1 Reason, J. (1997). *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents*. Aldershot: Ashgate.

- **Bibliographic details:** xvii + 252 pp. ISBN 1840141042 (hb), 1840141050 (pbk). Routledge reissue, DOI https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315543543.
- **Read:** book description only.
  - The bibliographic data come from the Internet Archive MARC record (item `managingrisksofo0000reas`) and Open Library.
  - The contents come from the Routledge product page (https://www.routledge.com/Managing-the-Risks-of-Organizational-Accidents/Reason/p/book/9781840141054). It lists ten chapters, the ninth being "Engineering a safety culture", followed by "Reconciling the different approaches to safety management".
- **Not read:** the text.
  - The Internet Archive copy is lending-only, and Taylor & Francis blocked access.
  - A Lund University course copy of chapter 9 turned up in a search, but the file now returns 404.
  - **The page numbers of chapter 9 could not be verified.**
- **What matters:**
  - GAIN (2004, §2.1) attributes to this book the five-part model the paper uses (informed, reporting, just, flexible and learning cultures) and the definition the paper quotes ("an atmosphere of trust in which people are encouraged (even rewarded)…").
  - That definition does not appear in Reason (1998) (§1.2 below; checked by text search). Quoting it from Reason himself needs the book.
- **Where it fits:** §2.1, first paragraph. Get a print copy and note the chapter-9 pages for the five components and the just-culture definition. Then cite Reason directly instead of "GAIN Working Group E's summary of Reason".

### 1.2 Reason, J. (1998). Achieving a safe culture: theory and practice. *Work & Stress*, 12(3), 293–306.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/02678379808256868
- **Read:** full text, the published version (page header "WORK & STRESS, 1998, VOL. 12, NO. 3 293-306"). The copy is hosted by the Royal Aeronautical Society Human Factors Group (http://www.raes-hfg.com/reports/21may09-Potential/21may09-JReason.pdf). Semantic Scholar lists it as the open copy. Cite the journal, not the host.
- **What matters:** Section 6, "Can a safe culture be socially engineered?" (pp. 302–305), states the chapter-9 argument in Reason's own words, in a peer-reviewed journal, with page numbers.
  - **Informed culture (p. 294):** "In most important respects an informed culture is a safety culture." This settles audit item n19: reporting and just culture are the prerequisites of an informed culture, not five parallel parts.
  - **What makes people report (p. 302):** Reason gives O'Leary and Chappell's features of successful reporting systems: "confidentiality or the de-identification of reporters; the separation of the agency or department collecting and analysing the reports from those bodies with authority to institute disciplinary proceedings and impose sanctions; rapid, useful, accessible and intelligible feedback to the reporting community; and making it easy for reporters to complete and file reports". He adds that "the single most important factor is trust".
  - **Against "no blame" (p. 303):** a "'no blame' culture, implying a blanket amnesty on all types of unsafe behaviour", is "neither feasible nor desirable". A culture in which all acts are immune from punishment "would lack credibility in the eyes of the workforce".
  - **The line (p. 303):** everyone should understand "where the line must be drawn between unacceptable behaviour, deserving of disciplinary action, and the remainder, where punishment is neither appropriate nor helpful in furthering the cause of safety".
  - **Marx's principle (p. 303):** "neither the error nor the magnitude of its consequences should be the focus of the subsequent investigation. Rather, it is the individual's underlying conduct at the time the error was committed that is the proper subject of the review."
  - **Necessary violations (p. 304):** these "arise largely from inadequacies of the equipment or workplace", and responsibility for them "lies more with the organization at large than with the individuals concerned".
  - **The substitution test (pp. 304–305):** Johnston's test asks whether a comparable person would have acted differently "In the light of how the events unfolded and were perceived by those involved in real time".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.1:** replaces the GAIN-mediated account with Reason's own, including the no-blame point now cited to GAIN §1.4.
  - **§2.6 S5 and S6:** the separation of collection from discipline, and feedback, in Reason's own words.
  - **P4:** credibility of the line.
  - **P2 and §7:** "necessary violations" is a precise human analogue of reward hacks that exploit broken environments. Responsibility sits with the environment's designer, which supports fixing or withdrawing the environment rather than correcting the episode.
  - **Group 4:** the substitution test is a hindsight safeguard.

### 1.3 Marx, D. (2001). *Patient Safety and the "Just Culture": A Primer for Health Care Executives*. New York, NY: Trustees of Columbia University.

- **Read:**
  - **PSNet listing**, re-opened (https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/patient-safety-and-just-culture-primer-health-care-executives): "Four key behavior concepts serve as the structure for the paper: human error, negligence, reckless conduct, and knowing violations."
  - **Marx's 2007 PSNet interview** (https://psnet.ahrq.gov/perspective/conversation-withdavid-marx-jd), full text. The paper already cites it.
  - **Reason (1998, p. 303)**, which summarises Marx's 1997 *Ground Effects* articles (§1.2).
- **Not read:** the primer itself. The original MERS-TM link is dead. A Wayback Machine snapshot exists (archived 7 February 2025), but web.archive.org was unreachable from this environment.
- **What matters:** In the interview, Marx describes the field's starting point as "So early on, it was all about getting the report", before attention moved to judging behavioural choices. On the boundary between at-risk and reckless behaviour he asks "How do we draw these lines?"
- **Where it fits:** the Marx sentence in §2.1.
  - Cite the conduct-not-outcome principle to Marx as reported by Reason (1998, p. 303), a peer-reviewed primary, or obtain the primer (a librarian may find a copy).
  - The "getting the report" remark marks the step from protecting reports to judging choices. The paper's P1/P4 split sits on exactly that step.

### 1.4 Dekker, S., *Just Culture*: editions, and the current fourth edition (2025)

- **Editions found:** Open Library records (searched 4 November 2026) show *Just Culture* (Ashgate, 2007, ISBN 9780754672678; and 2012, ISBN 1409440605), *Just Culture: Restoring Trust and Accountability in Your Organization*, 3rd ed. (Taylor & Francis, 2016, ISBN 9781472475763), and *Restorative Just Culture* (CRC Press, 2025).
- **Current edition:** Dekker, S. (2025). *Restorative Just Culture: From Disciplinary Action to Meaningful Accountability* (4th ed.). CRC Press. DOI https://doi.org/10.1201/9781003531562 (resolves to Taylor & Francis). The Routledge page says "4th Edition", 208 pp.
- **Read:** book description only.
  - The Routledge product page (description and contents): https://www.routledge.com/Restorative-Just-Culture-From-Disciplinary-Action-to-Meaningful-Accountability-Fourth-Edition/Dekker/p/book/9781032868233
  - Dekker's own two-page *Restorative Just Culture Checklist*, marked "Public Domain" (https://sidneydekker.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/RestorativeJustCultureChecklist.pdf). It is the most accessible statement of his current argument.
- **What matters:**
  - **Contents:** ch. 2 "The limits of flowchart just cultures and disciplinary action"; ch. 5 "Retributive just culture"; ch. 7 "Restorative just culture"; ch. 8 "Safety reporting and open disclosure"; ch. 9 "When does a mistake stop being honest?"; ch. 10 "The criminalization of human error".
  - **Retributive and restorative.** The checklist contrasts a retributive just culture, which asks "What rule is broken?", "How bad is the breach?" and "What should consequences be?", with a restorative one, which asks "Who is hurt?", "What do they need?" and "Whose obligation is that?".
    - Retributive accountability is "backward-looking, finding the person to blame and imposing proportional sanctions"; an account is "something you settle or pay".
    - Restorative accountability is "forward-looking"; an account is "something you tell and learn from".
    - The checklist states: "A retributive just culture is linked with hiding incidents and an unwillingness to report and learn." (The checklist gives no evidence for this sentence.)
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.1.** The paper presents the Reason–Marx–EU "line" model as *the* just culture. Dekker now classes the flowchart version of that model as retributive. *Safety Science* reviewers will expect this to be acknowledged.
  - **§4.2–4.3.** "Tell and learn" versus "settle or pay" maps closely onto the paper's distinction between learning uses of disclosures (uses 3–4) and per-episode training signals (use 1).
  - **§7.** The paper borrows a retributive structure for its exceptions (P4) but its use rules are forward-looking. Saying so would sharpen the analogy.
  - **What to read:** chapters 2 and 8.

## 2. Peer-reviewed work on just culture and its critiques

### 2.1 Dekker, S. W. A. (2009). Just culture: who gets to draw the line? *Cognition, Technology & Work*, 11(3), 177–185.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10111-008-0110-7
- **Read:** abstract only, from the Griffith University repository record (Springer redirected to a login; Unpaywall lists no open copy).
- **What matters:** The abstract argues that a clear line between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour "falsely assumes that culpability inheres in the act". It goes on:
  - "The critical question is not where to draw the line, but who gets to draw it."
  - Different accounts of the same incident "generate different repertoires of countermeasures".
  - The issue is what accountability promotes justice and safety: "backward-looking and retributive, or forward-looking and change-oriented".
- **Where it fits:** §2.1 ends: "Where the line belongs remains debated; this paper borrows the structure of a bounded line and leaves its position open." That sentence currently stands in for the critique. Engage it here.
  - P4(iii) ("who decides") and P5 (a custodian separate from training) are direct answers to Dekker's question.
  - P2's class-wide fixes are his "forward-looking" accountability.

### 2.2 Dekker, S. W. A., & Breakey, H. (2016). "Just culture:" Improving safety by achieving substantive, procedural and restorative justice. *Safety Science*, 85, 187–193.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2016.01.018
- **Read:** abstract only, from the Griffith repository record (handle 10072/142600; ScienceDirect blocked; Unpaywall lists no open copy).
- **What matters:** The paper treats just culture as a response to "anxiety about blame-free approaches on the one hand, and a concern about people's willingness to keep reporting safety-related issues on the other". It distinguishes three kinds of justice:
  - **substantive justice:** whether "regulations, rules and procedures themselves are fair and legitimate";
  - **procedural justice:** which "sets down processes for determining rule-breaches, offers protections for the accused, and governs who should make such determinations";
  - **restorative justice:** which aims to "restore the status of the individual involved and heal relationships".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.6.** S2–S5 are procedural-justice devices, and naming them as such links the paper to this literature.
  - **Substantive justice** has a clear AI counterpart: whether the reward specification itself is sound, because hacks of broken environments are partly a rule problem. This supports P2's environment fixes.
  - **P4 and P5:** who decides, in advance.
  - **§2.6, the gap:** restorative justice has no counterpart for models. Say so as a further disanalogy.

### 2.3 Cromie, S., & Bott, F. (2016). Just culture's "line in the sand" is a shifting one; an empirical investigation of culpability determination. *Safety Science*, 86, 258–272.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2016.03.012
- **Read:** bibliographic record and secondary summary.
  - Citation from Cromie's Trinity College Dublin research profile; authors confirmed in Semantic Scholar; the DOI resolves.
  - Abstract **not seen**: ScienceDirect and ResearchGate blocked access, Semantic Scholar says the publisher withholds it, and Unpaywall shows it as closed.
  - Content is known only from Woodlock's (2022, p. 445) summary, read in full (§2.6).
- **What matters:** As Woodlock summarises it:
  - Cromie and Bott surveyed a large sample of personnel in an aircraft maintenance company at six sites in Europe and North America.
  - "Respondents were found to consider just culture principles in their culpability determination, where 'who gets to draw the line' was a critical factor."
  - Proposed discipline differed: "North American sites found to be more severe than European (culture), engineers and managers more lenient than operational personnel (job role), and experienced staff more lenient than their juniors".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.1 and P4.** This is the main empirical *Safety Science* test of culpability judgement in aviation. It shows the line moving with who judges, which supports P4's insistence that bounds be fixed in advance and applied as written.
  - **Paper 07.** It is also the natural bridge to your paper 07, on outcome bias in AI models applying a culpability decision tree.
  - **Before citing:** read the full text for the sample size, design and results.

### 2.4 Schuit, I., & Scott, S. (2021). Perceptions of just culture between pilots and managers. *Aviation Psychology and Applied Human Factors*.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1027/2192-0923/a000208 (published online 11 August 2021; volume and pages not verified)
- **Read:** abstract only, from the Semantic Scholar record (Hogrefe blocked).
- **What matters:** The study used interviews and surveys within several airlines. It found that "decisions about culpability, the line between acceptable an [sic] unacceptable behavior, and the result of introducing more consequences are perceived differently by pilots and managers". It frames trust and the functioning of the reporting system as "critical for the success of a just safety culture".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.1:** a second aviation study showing the line is contested.
  - **P5, the human layer:** if staff and managers at an AI developer disagree about what will be treated as a breach, protection commitments need to be written down and checkable.

### 2.5 Heraghty, D., Rae, A. J., & Dekker, S. W. A. (2020) and Heraghty, D., Dekker, S. W. A., & Rae, A. (2021)

- **Citations:**
  - Heraghty, D., Rae, A. J., & Dekker, S. W. A. (2020). Managing accidents using retributive justice mechanisms: When the just culture policy gets done to you. *Safety Science*, 126, 104677. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2020.104677
  - Heraghty, D., Dekker, S. W. A., & Rae, A. (2021). Modifying an accident process and its justice system – From single narratives and retribution to multiple stories and restoration. *Safety Science*, 139, 105248. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2021.105248
- **Read:** abstracts only, from the Griffith repository records. The repository holds no full-text files, although Unpaywall lists the records as green open access. The setting is given in Heraghty's Griffith thesis abstract (https://doi.org/10.25904/1912/4352): a construction organisation on a UK nuclear new-build site.
- **What matters:**
  - **2020:** across five accidents, retributive mechanisms "negatively impacts three key areas; (1) the mental health of the individual; (2) organisational learning and; (3) organisational performance". The language of the analysis affected "the willingness to participate".
  - **2021:** follows "the design, introduction, and eventual sun-setting of a restorative justice process". It found that "restorative justice mechanisms can improve honesty, engagement and learning", but "factors external to the formal accident response process have a considerable influence".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.5:** case-study evidence, with no control group.
  - **§7 and P4/P6:** the 2021 study shows a protective regime being withdrawn under outside pressure. That is an argument for P4's written, advance commitments and for P6's measurement.

### 2.6 Woodlock, J. (2022). Procedural justice for all? Legitimacy, just culture and legal anxiety in European civil aviation. *Law & Society Review*, 56, 441–476.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/lasr.12622
- **Read:** full text (open access, CC BY; PDF from Cambridge Core).
- **What matters:**
  - **Design:** a survey of licensed aircraft maintenance engineers in Norway, Sweden and Portugal under Regulation 376/2014. There were 227 responses, a 19% response rate (p. 448); the analysed commercial air transport sample was N = 187 (p. 449).
  - **Main finding:** perceived procedural justice was more strongly associated with legitimacy than with legal anxiety. "Legal anxiety" was most influential in Portugal, where "34% of Portuguese LAMEs answered that they never use the occurrence reporting system, a result that is 11 times higher than Swedish or Norwegian respondents (3%)" (p. 451).
  - **Critique of retributive just culture (p. 442):** such programmes are criticised for assuming "a priori legitimacy" of existing rules.
  - **Limitations:** the study is described as "an exploratory venture" with a low response rate (p. 461).
  - **Cromie and Bott:** p. 445 summarises Cromie and Bott (2016); see §2.3.
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.3:** one EU law, applied unevenly. Reporting under the same Article 16 protection differs by national legal culture.
  - **§2.5:** cross-sectional evidence.
  - **P5:** staff at AI developers may feel legal anxiety even where models cannot.

### 2.7 Dekker, S. (2011). The criminalization of human error in aviation and healthcare: A review. *Safety Science*, 49(2), 121–127.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2010.09.010
- **Read:** abstract only, from the Griffith repository record (closed access according to Unpaywall).
- **What matters:** The abstract treats rising criminal prosecution as "a threat to the health and safety of employees and entire safety-critical systems". It notes that countermeasures "are local and haphazard" and examines "organizational consequences for disclosure and incident reporting". It closes by proposing research directions, which signals that the evidence base is thin.
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.3:** Art. 16(6) protects reporters "Without prejudice to applicable national criminal law"; this review documents why that carve-out matters.
  - **§2.5:** an honest note that there is little hard evidence on how prosecution affects reporting.
  - **Table 1, last-but-one row ("No statute protects the channel"):** even in aviation, statutory protection is incomplete.

### 2.8 Wachter, R. M., & Pronovost, P. J. (2009). Balancing "no blame" with accountability in patient safety. *New England Journal of Medicine*, 361(14), 1401–1406.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsb0903885
- **Read:** secondary summary only. PubMed has no abstract and NEJM blocked access; the summary is AHRQ PSNet's (https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/balancing-no-blame-accountability-patient-safety).
- **What matters:** PSNet says the authors discuss "why enforcement of safety standards tends to be lax (particularly in cases involving physicians)". ## 3. Empirical research on reporting and under-reporting

### Aviation

### 3.1 McMurtrie, K. J., & Molesworth, B. R. C. (2021). The impact of a legally defined just culture on voluntary reporting of safety information. *Aviation Psychology and Applied Human Factors*, 11(2), 88–97.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1027/2192-0923/a000215
- **Read:** full text: the publisher's PDF deposited in UNSWorks, licence CC BY-NC-ND (http://hdl.handle.net/1959.4/unsworks_82317).
- **What matters:**
  - **Design:** 539 European-based pilots, compared with an earlier Australian sample.
  - **Results:** "84% and 57% of the European and Australian pilots, respectively, trust their airlines' just culture"; "53% of the Australian pilots and 33% of the European pilots stated they had failed to report, or had under-reported, safety information". Fear of reprisal was the leading reason for not reporting among Australians, and a major one among Europeans (Table 4).
  - **The earlier Australian study,** as described here, found that about a third of pilots who did report "withheld information in their report (i.e., partially report)" because of that fear.
  - **Caveat:** the authors state that whether the EU's legal definition explains the difference "is unknown", and they flag possible self-selection bias.
  - **A counterpoint the paper should acknowledge:** the authors note that North Asia had the lowest accident rates in 2015–2019 "despite a strong and overt punishment culture".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.5:** direct evidence on pilots' reasons for not reporting (cross-sectional, self-reported).
  - **§7, "Disclosure is incomplete and can be strategic":** partial reporting is the human counterpart of a model confessing minor faults while omitting major ones (Golchin and Wetter, already cited).

### 3.2 European Commission, DG MOVE (2020). *Ex-post evaluation of Regulation (EU) No 376/2014 on the reporting, analysis and follow-up of occurrences in civil aviation: Executive summary*.

- **Bibliographic details:** Written by V. Ludden et al. (Ramboll, ALG, DeepBlue, DLA Piper), March 2020. Publications Office, ISBN 978-92-76-26978-6, DOI https://doi.org/10.2832/223993.
- **Read:** full text of the 17-page executive summary, downloaded from the Publications Office record (https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/2a149e57-3b62-11eb-b27b-01aa75ed71a1). The full study report was not read.
- **What matters:**
  - **Reports doubled (p. ix):** "the number of occurrence records contained in the ECR more than doubled, from 1,125,264 at the end of 2014 to 2,548,578 reports in Q3 of 2019."
  - **But mandatory reporting rose too:** the evaluators attribute much of the rise to the Regulation because "it made standardised reporting, analysis and follow-up mandatory for a range of actors that were not yet required to do so".
  - **Just culture lags (p. xi):** "Only six Member States have put the necessary rules in place to ensure that their designated 'just culture' bodies are adequately empowered" under Art. 16(6), (9) and (11). Causes include "a lack of judicial buy-in, and ingrained behaviours".
- **Where it fits:**
  - **§2.5:** the Danish confound (protection introduced together with mandatory reporting) recurs at EU scale. Cite both.
  - **§2.3 and S6:** the review that the paper presents as feedback (Art. 16(13)) exists, and it found Article 16 only partly implemented.

### 3.3 U.S. DOT Office of Inspector General (2012). *Long Term Success of ATSAP Will Require Improvements in Oversight, Accountability, and Transparency* (Report AV-2012-152, 19 July 2012).

- **URL:** https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/Report%20on%20FAA%27s%20Air%20Traffic%20Safety%20Action%20Program%5E7-19-12.pdf
- **Read:** full text.
- **What matters:** ATSAP, the controllers' counterpart of ASAP, was launched in July 2008. The report finds:
  - **Intake (p. 4):** "As of December 31, 2011, FAA has collected more than 41,000 reports, and the program shows promise as a tool to promote increased safety reporting."
  - **Unverified reports (p. 4):** "unknown events, approximately 50 percent of all ATSAP event reports, are not verified for accuracy before being accepted".
  - **Sole-source scope (p. 8):** the ATSAP MOU "is much more generous than ASAP MOUs in regard to what qualifies as 'sole-source'". This "could bolster confidence in ATSAP's confidentiality, it also prevents facility management from becoming aware of potential safety problems".
  - **Conduct reports and the amnesty perception (p. 12):** reports of controllers "falling asleep, viewing a personal video player" were accepted, which "may lead to the incorrect perception that ATSAP is an amnesty program where reports are automatically accepted".

### 3.4 U.S. DOT Office of Inspector General (2013). *FAA's Efforts to Track and Mitigate Air Traffic Losses of Separation Are Limited by Data Collection and Implementation Challenges* (Report AV-2013-046, 27 February 2013).

- **URL:** https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/LoSS%20Final%202-27-13_final_signed_rev.pdf
- **Read:** full text.
- **What matters:**
  - **The rise (p. 6):** reported operational errors rose "53 percent, from 1,234 to 1,887" between fiscal years 2009 and 2010. The FAA attributed this mostly to more reporting through ATSAP and TARP, an automated loss-of-separation detection system.
  - **The OIG's finding (p. 3):** "the increase in reported errors was linked, in part, to a rise in actual errors rather than increased reporting". En-route centres, which had long used automated detection, also rose "from 353 to 489, an increase of 39 percent" (p. 7). A waiver revocation and reclassification "accounted for 23 percent of the increase" (p. 8).
  - **Earlier reports (Exhibit C, p. 21):** a 2005 OIG investigation found Dallas/Fort Worth TRACON managers "underreported operational errors over a seven-year period", and that "management created an atmosphere of self-reporting and other incentives that discouraged employees from identifying operational errors". A 2004 report found that the FAA relied on self-reporting and had no system "to verify that this reporting process is reliable".
- **Where §§3.3–3.4 fit:** this is the closest real aviation case to the paper's split between the disclosure channel and the detection channel: ATSAP (protected self-reports) plus TARP (automated, independent detection).
  - **§2.5:** protected reporting raised intake, but changes in self-report counts could not be read without an independent detector.
  - **§4.3 and S3:** widening the "sole-source" category shrank the independent route.
  - **H4 and P4:** accepting conduct reports produced an "amnesty" perception, a real moral-hazard concern.
  - **P6:** DFW shows years of hidden under-reporting where only self-report existed. That is the paper's disclosure-recall argument, in aviation.
  - **§5:** it could carry the expanded aviation section.
  - **Caveat:** these are audits, not controlled studies. FAA responses are appended to both reports. The FAA partially concurred with some recommendations in both, and in 2012 did not concur with two.

### Healthcare and occupational safety

### 3.5 Archer, S., Hull, L., Soukup, T., Mayer, E., Athanasiou, T., Sevdalis, N., & Darzi, A. (2017). Development of a theoretical framework of factors affecting patient safety incident reporting: a theoretical review of the literature. *BMJ Open*, 7(12), e017155.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-017155
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 29284714).
- **What matters:** 110 articles (more than 29,726 participants) gave 748 barriers. "The top two barriers cited were fear of adverse consequences (161, representing 21.52% of barriers) and process and systems of reporting". The figures are frequency counts of barriers mentioned, not effect sizes.
- **Where it fits:** §2.5, as one sentence: fear of consequences is the most commonly cited barrier in healthcare reporting research.

### 3.6 Evans, S. M., Berry, J. G., Smith, B. J., Esterman, A., Selim, P., O'Shaughnessy, J., & DeWit, M. (2006). Attitudes and barriers to incident reporting: a collaborative hospital study. *Quality & Safety in Health Care*, 15(1), 39–43.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/qshc.2004.012559
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 16456208).
- **What matters:** In an anonymous survey of 186 doctors and 587 nurses in six South Australian hospitals, "The most frequently stated barrier to reporting for doctors and nurses was lack of feedback (57.7% and 61.8% agreeing, respectively)". Near misses were least likely to be reported.
- **Where it fits:** S6 and P6. Fear is not the only barrier; feedback matters, as Reason (1998, p. 302) also says. It is a useful qualification in §2.5.

### 3.7 Hutchinson, A., Young, T. A., Cooper, K. L., McIntosh, A., Karnon, J. D., Scobie, S., & Thomson, R. G. (2009). Trends in healthcare incident reporting and relationship to safety and quality data in acute hospitals: results from the National Reporting and Learning System. *Quality & Safety in Health Care*, 18(1), 5–10.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/qshc.2007.022400
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 19204125).
- **What matters:** Across all acute hospitals in England, "Higher reporting rates correlated with positive data on safety culture" and with better risk-management ratings. There was "no apparent association" with standardised mortality ratios. The evidence is longitudinal and correlational.
- **Where it fits:** P3 and P6. A high report count signals a healthy reporting culture, not an unsafe organisation, which supports the paper's warning against treating a falling count of flagged chains of thought as success.

### 3.8 Probst, T. M., Brubaker, T. L., & Barsotti, A. (2008). Organizational injury rate underreporting: the moderating effect of organizational safety climate. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 93(5), 1147–1154.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.5.1147
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 18808232).
- **What matters:** The study covered 38 contractors on one construction site, with safety-climate data from 1,390 employees.
  - It compared injuries reported to OSHA with insurance medical-claims data, an independent record. "While the annual injury rate reported to OSHA was 3.11 injuries per 100 workers, the rate of eligible injuries that were not reported to OSHA was 10.90 injuries per 100 employees."
  - Firms with poor safety climate left "81% of eligible injuries unreported", against 47% where the climate was positive.
- **Where it fits:**
  - **P6:** this is the human-safety counterpart of measuring disclosure recall against independent evidence. Under-reporting is measured, not self-reported.
  - **§2.5:** the strongest under-reporting design in this pack, though the link to climate is correlational.

### 3.9 Stavropoulou, C., Doherty, C., & Tosey, P. (2015). How effective are incident-reporting systems for improving patient safety? A systematic literature review. *Milbank Quarterly*, 93(4), 826–866.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0009.12166
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 26626987).
- **What matters:** Across 43 studies the review "did not find strong evidence that IRSs performed better than other methods". It found some single-loop learning but "little evidence of either improvements in outcomes or changes in the latent managerial factors involved in error production".
- **Where it fits:** §2.5 and §4.3. Protected reports pay off only when acted on; for models, that means use 3 (class-wide fixes). It also tempers any claim that reporting systems are proven to improve safety.

### 3.10 Kaur, M., de Boer, R. J., Oates, A., Rafferty, J., & Dekker, S. (2019). Restorative just culture: a study of the practical and economic effects of implementing restorative justice in an NHS trust. *MATEC Web of Conferences*, 273, 01007.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1051/matecconf/201927301007 (the volume and article number are read from the DOI)
- **Read:** abstract only, from the Semantic Scholar record (the MATEC site blocked access, although the paper is open access).
- **What matters:** At Mersey Care NHS Trust (from 2016), restorative just culture "coincided with" "a reduction in suspensions and dismissals, increase in the reporting of adverse events". The authors "conservatively attribute half of these savings to the introduction of a just and learning culture itself". The design is a single-site before–after study, with no control.
- **Where it fits:** §2.5, as the most-cited healthcare implementation, with its causal limits stated.

### What this evidence can and cannot show

- **No controlled studies found.** The search found no randomised or controlled study of protection versus punishment on reporting, in aviation or healthcare, and no controlled study of how prosecuting pilots or controllers affects reporting. Dekker (2011) treats the latter as a research agenda.
- **What the evidence is:**
  - cross-sectional surveys of stated reasons and self-reported non-reporting (§§2.4, 2.6, 3.1, 3.5, 3.6);
  - before–after case studies, each confounded: the EU, where mandatory reporting rose at the same time; ATSAP, where detection tools and reclassification changed together; Mersey Care, a single site;
  - correlational studies (§§3.7, 3.8);
  - audits (§§3.3, 3.4).
- **The strongest design** is Probst et al.'s comparison of reported against independently recorded injuries.
- **What it supports:** the paper's statement in §2.5 that aviation's lesson is "an institutional consensus supported by case evidence" holds. It can now rest on a body of references rather than a single Danish case, and the point that AI training permits controlled tests becomes stronger.

## 4. Hindsight and outcome knowledge in judging reports

### 4.1 Fischhoff, B. (1975). Hindsight ≠ foresight: the effect of outcome knowledge on judgment under uncertainty. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, 1(3), 288–299.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.1.3.288
- **Reprint:** *Quality & Safety in Health Care* (2003), 12(4), 304–311 (PubMed 12897366).
- **Read:** abstract only, from the PubMed record of the reprint.
- **What matters:** Outcome knowledge increased the judged likelihood of reported outcomes. "Judges were, however, largely unaware of the effect that outcome knowledge had on their perceptions", and so "overestimated what they would have known without outcome knowledge".

### 4.2 Baron, J., & Hershey, J. C. (1988). Outcome bias in decision evaluation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 54(4), 569–579.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 3367280).
- **What matters:** Across five studies, people rated the same decision as better reasoned when it turned out well. "Although subjects who were asked felt that they should not consider outcomes in making these evaluations, they did so."

### 4.3 Caplan, R. A., Posner, K. L., & Cheney, F. W. (1991). Effect of outcome on physician judgments of appropriateness of care. *JAMA*, 265(15), 1957–1960.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1991.03460150061024 (resolves to JAMA)
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 2008024).
- **What matters:** 112 anaesthesiologists rated matched case pairs that differed only in outcome severity, with cases randomly assigned. "the proportion of ratings for appropriate care decreased by 31 percentage points when the outcome was changed from temporary to permanent". This is controlled evidence that peer review of the same conduct depends on outcome.

### 4.4 Lawton, R., & Parker, D. (2002). Barriers to incident reporting in a healthcare system. *Quality & Safety in Health Care*, 11(1), 15–18.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/qhc.11.1.15
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 12078362).
- **What matters:** In a scenario experiment with 315 doctors, nurses and midwives, outcome was varied across versions. Reporting to a senior colleague was "most likely when the incident represents the violation of a protocol with a bad outcome". Doctors were especially reluctant to report.
- **Note:** this bears on group 3 too. Outcome shapes whether people report, not only how reports are judged.

### 4.5 Henriksen, K., & Kaplan, H. (2003). Hindsight bias, outcome knowledge and adaptive learning. *Quality & Safety in Health Care*, 12(Suppl 2), ii46–ii50.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/qhc.12.suppl_2.ii46
- **Read:** abstract only (PubMed 14645895).
- **What matters:** "Investigations that are anchored to outcome knowledge run the risk of not capturing the complexities and uncertainties facing sharp end personnel and why their actions made sense at the time." The paper discusses ways to reduce the effect.

### Where group 4 fits

- **§2.1.** The paper says the line is drawn "by the type of behaviour, not by the outcome", citing UC Davis PSNet. These studies show why that is hard: judges who know the outcome rate the same conduct differently and do not notice they are doing it.
  - Reason's substitution test, "as perceived … in real time" (§1.2), and Marx's conduct-not-consequences rule (§1.3) are the field's safeguards.
- **P4 and P6.** Monitors and judges, human or model, that see outcomes may judge disclosures more harshly when outcomes are bad.
  - The confession judge in Joglekar et al. sees the answer and the tool-call transcript (§3.6).
  - Your own paper 07 tests outcome bias in AI models applying a culpability tree; cite it here.
- **§7, "The intent line does not transfer".** Add that outcome-driven judgement is a further reason to prefer response-type bounds.

## Suggested changes to the paper

1. **§2.1, first paragraph: cite Reason directly.**
   - Replace "In GAIN Working Group E's summary of Reason" with Reason (1997, ch. 9 "Engineering a safety culture", pages from your print copy) for the five components and the just-culture definition.
   - Add Reason (1998) with page numbers for:
     - an informed culture as "in most important respects" the safety culture (p. 294);
     - trust as "the single most important factor" (p. 302);
     - the no-blame critique (p. 303), which can then replace GAIN §1.4.

   This also settles audit item n19.
2. **§2.1, Marx sentence: cite the principle through a primary.**
   - Cite the conduct-not-consequences principle to Marx as reported in Reason (1998, p. 303), so it rests on a peer-reviewed primary rather than PSNet alone.
   - Optionally add Marx's "it was all about getting the report" (PSNet 2007) to mark the step from protecting reports to judging choices, which is where P1 and P4 divide.
3. **§2.1, final two sentences: engage the critique.** Replace "Where the line belongs remains debated…" with a short paragraph built on:
   - Dekker (2009): who draws the line;
   - Dekker and Breakey (2016): substantive, procedural and restorative justice;
   - Cromie and Bott (2016), via Woodlock (2022, p. 445), and Schuit and Scott (2021): the line moves with role, site, experience and seniority;
   - Dekker (2025, chs. 2, 5, 7): "flowchart" just culture as retributive.

   Then say how the paper answers it: bounds fixed in advance (P4), by type of response, decided by a party other than the training team (P4(iii), P5). That turns an admitted gap into a contribution.
4. **§2.5: rebuild the evidence subsection around a small evidence table** (study, setting, design, finding, causal limit), using:
   - aviation: McMurtrie and Molesworth (2021), Woodlock (2022), the EU ex-post evaluation (2020);
   - healthcare and occupational safety: Archer et al. (2017), Evans et al. (2006), Probst et al. (2008), Hutchinson et al. (2009), Stavropoulou et al. (2015), Kaur et al. (2019), Heraghty et al. (2020, 2021).

   Keep the paper's conclusion, but state the design limits explicitly, and add that the EU's doubling of reports repeats the Danish confound.
5. **New short subsection (§2.6 or §5): ATSAP and TARP as an aviation precedent for disclosure and detection channels.** Use OIG (2012, 2013):
   - protected reporting raised intake;
   - automated detection was needed to interpret the 53% rise;
   - a broad sole-source definition weakened the independent route;
   - accepting conduct reports created an "amnesty" perception;
   - DFW under-reported for seven years under self-report alone.

   Link these to S3, P2, P6, H4 and the §4.3 sole-source rule. This is the strongest available addition to the aviation payoff.
6. **P6 and P3: give the measurement claims human-safety precedents.**
   - Cite Probst et al. (2008) for measuring under-reporting against an independent record. It is the human counterpart of disclosure recall.
   - Cite Hutchinson et al. (2009) for higher reporting rates going with a better safety culture. That supports the claim that a falling count of flagged disclosures is not a success metric.
7. **§2.1, P4 and §7: add hindsight and outcome bias.**
   - Cite Fischhoff (1975), Baron and Hershey (1988), Caplan et al. (1991), Lawton and Parker (2002) and Henriksen and Kaplan (2003) where the paper says the line follows behaviour, not outcome.
   - Note that judges and monitors, human or model, who see outcomes are vulnerable to the same bias; cite your paper 07, and Oğuz et al. (2026) once read.
   - Consider adding to P4 or P6 that evaluation of disclosures should be outcome-blind where it can be.
8. **§2.3 and the Table 1 row on statutory protection: treat criminal law as a known weakness.**
   - Use Dekker (2011), Woodlock (2022) and the EU evaluation ("lack of judicial buy-in"; only six Member States with empowered just-culture bodies).
   - Show that Art. 16(6)'s "without prejudice to applicable national criminal law" is a recognised weak point, not a footnote. This also strengthens the disanalogy in Table 1 ("No statute protects the channel").
9. **§5, aviation paragraph: expand it into a short section on AI tools in aviation safety management.** Build it on Kirwan (2024), Macrae (2022), ATSAP/TARP and Reg. 376/2014 Arts. 15–16. Possible topics, for you to judge from operational experience:
   - AI logs and self-assessments as occurrence or FDM-type data inside the SMS;
   - a named custodian for them;
   - whether an AI tool's self-flagged error counts as a "report", and who is protected;
   - keeping such data out of retraining for the behaviour it describes.
10. **§7, "Protection could leave real misbehaviour uncorrected": answer it with the field's own response to "no blame".**
    - Healthcare's answer (Wachter and Pronovost, 2009) was accountability for repeated breaches of established, independently checkable standards. Reason (1998, p. 303) argued that blanket amnesty lacks credibility.
    - Both fit P2 (correct on independent evidence) rather than training on disclosures.
    - Add Reason's "necessary violations" (p. 304) and Dekker and Breakey's substantive justice to argue that hacks of broken environments call for fixing the environment, which the paper already recommends.
