Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Just Culture for Machines: Why Penalising Disclosure Teaches Concealment", for publication in *Safety Science* as a research paper.

Safety science has established that when reports of error feed punishment, reporting falls and hazards stay hidden, and aviation's protected reporting systems (ASRS, ASAP, flight data monitoring, EU Regulation 376/2014, ICAO Annex 19) are among its best-known answers. The manuscript argues that the training of AI systems now faces the same incentive problem in a sharper form. Developers increasingly oversee AI systems through what the systems disclose about their own behaviour: their written reasoning, self-reports and "confessions". When those disclosures set the penalty for the behaviour they reveal, training can teach the system to conceal instead of to behave, and recent experiments report exactly that.

The paper maps the structure of aviation's protected reporting onto AI training. It states the conditions under which disclosure-based penalties cannot make good behaviour outscore concealed misbehaviour, and turns aviation's institutions into six design principles and five falsifiable predictions. It also sets out where the analogy fails. I believe it will interest *Safety Science* readers for two reasons: it applies a core safety-science concept to a technology that is entering safety-critical work, aviation included, and it shows that controlled experiments in AI training could test a mechanism that aviation could only observe.

The manuscript is original. It has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. An earlier version was posted as a preprint in the author's public research repository. Generative AI was used in its preparation, as declared in the manuscript, and the author takes full responsibility for its content. The author is the founder of CitadelAero, which builds AI tools for aviation; this is declared on the title page. The paper is a theoretical analysis and uses no data.
