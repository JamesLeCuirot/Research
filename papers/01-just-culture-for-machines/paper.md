abstract: |
  Aviation learned that when reports of error feed punishment, people stop reporting and the hazards stay hidden. Its response was a set of protected reporting arrangements, among them NASA's Aviation Safety Reporting System, the FAA's Aviation Safety Action Program and flight data programmes, EU Regulation 376/2014 and ICAO Annex 19. These separate the channel through which people disclose from the channel through which they are held to account, and they exclude wilful misconduct and gross negligence from protection. This paper argues that the training of AI systems faces the same incentive structure in formal terms. When a model's own disclosures (its chain of thought, self-reports or confessions) set the reward or penalty for the behaviour they disclose, optimisation acts on the disclosure as well as on the behaviour, and concealment is often the cheaper way to raise reward. A simple argument shows that a penalty computed only from a disclosure can never make good behaviour outscore concealed misbehaviour. Experiments in which reasoning models were trained against chain-of-thought monitors show the predicted obfuscation. OpenAI's confession method, which rewards a confession only for its honesty and keeps that reward apart from the task reward, is in effect a just-culture design. The paper translates just-culture practice into six design principles for training and monitoring AI systems, gives five falsifiable predictions with an experimental design, and sets out where the analogy fails. Models are not moral agents, and the mechanism is optimisation pressure rather than fear.

# 1 Introduction

Developers of large language models increasingly rely on what models say about their own behaviour. Reasoning models write a *chain of thought* (CoT): intermediate reasoning in natural language, produced before a final answer or action. Automated *monitors*, usually other language models, can read these traces and flag signs of intent to misbehave. In one study of a frontier reasoning model trained on coding tasks, a CoT monitor caught 95% of two widespread exploits, against 60% for a monitor that saw only the model's actions (Baker et al., 2025, Table 1). Models can also be asked, after a task, to report on their own conduct. OpenAI's "confessions" are one such report (Joglekar et al., 2025). This paper calls all of these *disclosures*: information about a model's behaviour that comes from the model itself.

Once a disclosure reveals misbehaviour, it is tempting to use it to remove the misbehaviour, for example by lowering the reward of any training episode whose chain of thought a monitor flags. Baker et al. (2025) did this. At low levels of optimisation the result was a better model. With more optimisation, "agents learn obfuscated reward hacking, hiding their intent within the CoT while still exhibiting a significant rate of reward hacking" (Baker et al., 2025, abstract). The monitor's recall fell to near zero while the cheating continued (§3.2).

Aviation safety professionals will recognise the shape of this problem. If reports of mistakes are used to punish the people who make them, fewer mistakes are reported and the hazards behind them stay hidden. Over several decades aviation built institutions to prevent this: NASA's Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS), established in 1976; the FAA's Aviation Safety Action Program (ASAP) and Flight Operational Quality Assurance (FOQA); the European Union's occurrence reporting regulation; and ICAO's principles for protecting safety data. Under the name *just culture*, these protect people who report while keeping a line for gross negligence and wilful violations.

This paper argues that the parallel is structural, not merely rhetorical. A language model in training does not fear punishment. But when the consequence of a behaviour depends on whether the system disclosed it, optimisation acts on the disclosure. For a model, the pressure arrives as gradient updates rather than anticipated sanctions, and the result is the same: the cheapest way to avoid the consequence is often to stop disclosing.

The contributions are:

- A formal statement of the incentive structure shared by aviation reporting and AI training, with a simple argument that a penalty computed only from a disclosure can never make good behaviour outscore concealed misbehaviour (Section 4.1).
- A correspondence table from aviation reporting institutions to AI training and monitoring, with explicit points where the analogy breaks, and the identification of *disclosure-blind correction*, modelled on the FAA's "sole-source" rule, as the central design move (Sections 4.2–4.3).
- Six design principles for training and monitoring AI systems, including the human layer of reporting inside AI developers (Section 5).
- Five falsifiable predictions and an experimental design to test them (Section 6).
- A literature search that found no prior work linking just culture to chain-of-thought monitoring, confessions or training against monitors, and the observation that confession training already implements a just-culture design (Section 3.7).

This is a theory paper and reports no new experiments. Claims about AI systems rest on the published studies cited; many are preprints or research blog posts from AI developers and are labelled as such. Regulatory text is quoted as it stood on 3 November 2026, with its jurisdiction and status (binding rule or guidance) stated. Nothing here is legal or regulatory advice.

# 2 How aviation learned to protect reporting

## 2.1 Safety culture, reporting culture and just culture

Reason (1997) described safety culture as several interacting parts. In the summary prepared by the Global Aviation Information Network (GAIN) Working Group E (2004, Figure 1), these are an informed culture, a reporting culture ("An organizational climate in which people are prepared to report their errors and near-misses"), a just culture, a flexible culture and a learning culture. Reason described a just culture as "an atmosphere of trust in which people are encouraged (even rewarded) for providing essential safety-related information, but in which they are also clear about where the line must be drawn between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour" (as quoted in GAIN Working Group E, 2004, §2.1). In his foreword to the GAIN report he called engineering a just culture "the all-important early step; so much else depends upon it."

Two features of just culture matter for this paper. First, it is not a "no-blame" culture. GAIN Working Group E (2004, §1.4) put it directly: "A 'no-blame' culture is neither feasible nor desirable," and "A blanket amnesty on all unsafe acts would lack credibility in the eyes of employees and could be seen to oppose natural justice." Second, the line is drawn by the type of behaviour, not by the outcome. Marx (2001) organised his account around four behavioural concepts: human error, negligence, reckless conduct and knowing violations (as summarised by PSNet and by GAIN Working Group E, 2004, §2.3). Later healthcare practice commonly distinguishes "human error (e.g., slips), at-risk behavior (e.g., taking shortcuts), and reckless behavior", with the response "predicated on the type of behavior associated with the error, and not the severity of the event" (PSNet Editorial Team, 2024). Dekker (2025) questions the "flowchart" approach to deciding culpability and contrasts retributive with restorative just culture. Where exactly the line belongs remains debated. This paper borrows the structure of a bounded line and leaves its position open.

## 2.2 Protected reporting in the United States

**ASRS.** Under the FAA's Aviation Safety Reporting Program, pilots, controllers, cabin crew, maintenance personnel, dispatchers "or any other person" may report safety problems to NASA, which "serves as a third party to receive and process Aviation Safety Reports" (FAA, 2021, ¶1). The FAA chose NASA because this "would ensure the anonymity of the reporter and of all parties involved in a reported occurrence or incident and, consequently, increase the flow of information necessary for the effective evaluation of the safety and efficiency of the NAS" (FAA, 2021, ¶6.2). The FAA and NASA first executed their memorandum of agreement on 15 August 1975 (FAA, 2021, ¶6.2), and NASA dates the establishment of ASRS to April 1976 (NASA ASRS, n.d.). NASA administers the system and the FAA funds it (NASA ASRS, n.d.). NASA reports 2,321,050 reports received from April 1976 to December 2025, and an intake in 2025 of about 10,094 reports per month, against roughly 400 per month in the programme's first days (NASA ASRS, n.d.).

Three protections operate together. The first is a binding use restriction in US federal regulation:

> "The Administrator of the FAA will not use reports submitted to the National Aeronautics and Space Administration under the Aviation Safety Reporting Program (or information derived therefrom) in any enforcement action except information concerning accidents or criminal offenses which are wholly excluded from the Program." (14 C.F.R. § 91.25)

The second is a waiver of sanction, set out in FAA guidance (an advisory circular that states it does "not have the force and effect of law"; FAA, 2021, ¶2). Filing a report is treated as "indicative of a constructive attitude", and "although a finding of violation may be made, neither a civil penalty nor certificate suspension will be imposed" if four conditions hold: the violation was "inadvertent and not deliberate"; it did not involve a criminal offence, an accident or a competency action under 49 U.S.C. § 44709; the person has no violation found in the previous five years; and the person proves that they reported to NASA within ten days (FAA, 2021, ¶12.3). The third is confidentiality: NASA returns the identification strip of each report to the reporter and, except for accidents and criminal matters, "does not create or retain a copy" (FAA, 2021, ¶10.2).

The protection has a precise shape. It does not stop the regulator acting. "When a violation of 14 CFR comes to the attention of the FAA from a source other than a report filed with NASA under the ASRS, the Administrator of the FAA will take appropriate action" (FAA, 2021, ¶8.3). What ASRS prevents is the report itself becoming the evidence, and for inadvertent violations it removes the sanction from people who reported.

**ASAP.** The Aviation Safety Action Program is a voluntary reporting programme run by an operator under a memorandum of understanding with the FAA. Its guidance states: "Under an ASAP, safety issues are resolved through corrective action rather than through punishment or discipline" (FAA, 2020, ¶1). Reports are reviewed by an Event Review Committee (ERC) made up of a management representative, a representative of the employee labour association where there is one, and an FAA inspector (FAA, 2020, ¶A.8). Reports are excluded for intentional conduct, for reckless conduct ("a gross disregard for, or deliberate indifference to, safety or a safety standard") and for the "Big Five": "criminal activity, substance abuse, controlled substances, alcohol, or intentional falsification" (FAA, 2020, ¶¶18.2, 18.3, A.4). Corrective action may include "training, mentoring, counseling", and should not appear in official training records or personnel files (FAA, 2020, ¶15).

ASAP makes explicit a distinction this paper relies on. A report is *sole-source* "when all evidence of the event available is predicated on the ASAP report" (FAA, 2020, ¶A.20). When the FAA has evidence that does not come from the report, for example "reports from air traffic control (ATC) involving airspace deviations", it "investigates apparent violation(s) based on evidence independent of an ASAP report" (FAA, 2020, ¶16.4). The same paragraph adds:

> "no portion of the written ASAP report, including an ERC investigation or interview(s) concerning a reported event, is used to initiate or support company discipline or FAA enforcement action. [...] The company retains its authority to take disciplinary action based on information of an event independently obtained by the company (i.e., information not based or otherwise predicated upon the content of an employee's ASAP report)." (FAA, 2020, ¶16.4, Note)

**FOQA.** Flight Operational Quality Assurance is "an FAA-approved program for the routine collection and analysis of digital flight data gathered during aircraft operations" (14 C.F.R. § 13.401(b)(1)). Its data are also protected: "Except for criminal or deliberate acts, the Administrator will not use an operator's FOQA data or aggregate FOQA data in an enforcement action against that operator or its employees" when the data come from an approved programme (14 C.F.R. § 13.401(e)). Identifying data are normally removed, and a "gatekeeper", typically a line captain designated by the pilots' association, must concur before an individual crew can be contacted about an event (FAA, 2004, ¶8). The FAA's guidance also suggests that integrating FOQA data with programmes such as ASAP "should be considered to further enhance the safety value of the information" (FAA, 2004).

## 2.3 The European Union and the United Kingdom

Regulation (EU) No 376/2014 on occurrence reporting gives just culture a legal definition:

> "'just culture' means a culture in which front-line operators or other persons are not punished for actions, omissions or decisions taken by them that are commensurate with their experience and training, but in which gross negligence, wilful violations and destructive acts are not tolerated" (Regulation (EU) No 376/2014, Art. 2(12)).

Information from occurrence reports "shall be used only for the purpose for which it has been collected", and may not be used "in order to attribute blame or liability" or "for any purpose other than the maintenance or improvement of aviation safety" (Art. 15(2)). Member States "shall refrain from instituting proceedings in respect of unpremeditated or inadvertent infringements of the law which come to their attention only because they have been reported" (Art. 16(6)); report content shall not be used against reporters in disciplinary or administrative proceedings (Art. 16(7)); and reporters "shall not be subject to any prejudice by their employer" (Art. 16(9)). The protection is bounded:

> "The protection under paragraphs 6, 7 and 9 of this Article shall not apply to any of the following situations: (a) in cases of wilful misconduct; (b) where there has been a manifest, severe and serious disregard of an obvious risk and profound failure of professional responsibility to take such care as is evidently required in the circumstances, causing foreseeable damage to a person or property, or which seriously compromises the level of aviation safety." (Art. 16(10))

At the same time, the authorities "shall not be prevented from taking any action necessary for maintaining or improving aviation safety" (Art. 16(5)). Each organisation must adopt, after consulting staff representatives, internal rules describing how just-culture principles are guaranteed (Art. 16(11)). Each Member State designates a body to which staff may report alleged infringements of these rules, and staff "shall not be penalised for reporting alleged infringements" (Art. 16(12)). Member States must report to the Commission on the application of Article 16 every five years (Art. 16(13)). These provisions are binding EU law. In the United Kingdom the same provisions continue as assimilated law (UK Reg 376/2014), with the Civil Aviation Authority in the role of the Member State's authorities and designated body. Separately, EU air operations rules require a flight data monitoring programme for larger aeroplanes, which "shall be non-punitive and contain adequate safeguards to protect the source(s) of the data" (Commission Regulation (EU) No 965/2012, Annex III, ORO.AOC.130(b)).

## 2.4 ICAO Annex 19

ICAO Annex 19, *Safety Management*, sets international standards for State safety programmes and for the safety management systems (SMS) of service providers. EU law defines an SMS as "a systematic approach to managing aviation safety including the necessary organisational structures, accountabilities, policies and procedures" (Regulation (EU) No 376/2014, Art. 2(18)). The second edition of Annex 19 (ICAO, 2016) became applicable on 7 November 2019 and raised the protection of safety data to the status of standards in Appendix 3. A note to the Annex states the premise of the whole approach: "A reporting environment where employees and operational personnel may trust that their actions or omissions that are commensurate with their training and experience will not be punished is fundamental to safety reporting" (ICAO, 2016, 5.3.2, Note 1). Appendix 3 gives the rationale:

> "The protection of safety data, safety information and related sources is essential to ensure their continued availability, since the use of safety data and safety information for purposes other than maintaining or improving safety may inhibit the future availability of such data and information, with a significant adverse effect on safety." (ICAO, 2016, Appendix 3, Note 1)

States must ensure that safety data are not used for "disciplinary, civil, administrative and criminal proceedings against employees, operational personnel or organizations", for public disclosure, or for "any purposes other than maintaining or improving safety", unless a principle of exception applies (Appendix 3, §2.1). One exception arises where "there are facts and circumstances reasonably indicating that the occurrence may have been caused by an act or omission considered, in accordance with national laws, to be conduct constituting gross negligence, wilful misconduct or criminal activity" (Appendix 3, §3(a)). Two others require a competent authority to find that release is necessary for justice or for safety and that its benefits outweigh the likely harm to "the future collection and availability of safety data and safety information" (Appendix 3, §3(b)–(c)). States are not prevented from taking "any preventive, corrective or remedial action that is necessary to maintain or improve aviation safety" (5.3.4), and each safety data collection and processing system must have "a designated custodian to apply the protection" (Appendix 3, §5). Amendment 2 to Annex 19, adopted in June 2025, becomes applicable on 26 November 2026; in the amendment text it renumbers section 5.3 as 5.4 and makes minor wording changes, while leaving the Appendix 3 principles quoted above unchanged (ICAO, 2025).

## 2.5 What the evidence shows, and what it does not

The rationale for protection is stated plainly in the rules themselves: anonymity will "increase the flow of information" (FAA, 2021, ¶6.2), and misuse "may inhibit the future availability" of data (ICAO, 2016, Appendix 3, Note 1). Direct causal evidence is thinner. GAIN Working Group E (2004, §4.1) describes Denmark's 2002 law, which made reporting by air traffic controllers mandatory, confidential and non-punitive. In the first year Naviair, the Danish air navigation service provider, received 980 reports against 15 the year before, including 40–50 losses of separation against 15. This is a before-and-after comparison without a control group. Wei and Heim (2025) also caution that the ASRS model has not succeeded everywhere: a NASA-run confidential close-call system for US railways attracted limited industry participation. Aviation's lesson is therefore an institutional consensus supported by case evidence. It has not been established by controlled experiment, which is one reason AI training, where controlled experiments are possible, is a useful place to test it (Section 6).

## 2.6 The common structure

Across these instruments, six features recur:

- **S1 A designated disclosure channel** with defined protection (ASRS, ASAP, occurrence reports, FOQA/flight data monitoring).
- **S2 A use restriction.** Disclosures are not used to attribute blame or as evidence for sanctions, but may be used for safety action (14 C.F.R. § 91.25; Art. 15(2) and 16(5); Annex 19, 5.3.4).
- **S3 An independent route.** Evidence obtained without the report can still be used (FAA, 2021, ¶8.3; FAA, 2020, ¶16.4; Art. 16(6), "only because they have been reported").
- **S4 Bounded exceptions defined in advance.** Wilful misconduct, gross negligence, criminal acts and falsification sit outside protection.
- **S5 Separation of custody from enforcement.** NASA as a third party, the three-party ERC, the FOQA gatekeeper, the Annex 19 custodian, and GAIN's recommendation to separate "agency/department collecting and analyzing the reports from those bodies with the authority to institute disciplinary proceedings and impose sanctions" (GAIN Working Group E, 2004, §3.3).
- **S6 Feedback and measurement.** Report intake is tracked, findings are fed back, and the protection regime itself is reviewed (NASA ASRS, n.d.; Art. 16(13)).

# 3 The AI problem

## 3.1 Terms

Modern language models are refined by *reinforcement learning* (RL). The model, called the *policy*, produces outputs; a *reward* function scores them; and an optimiser adjusts the model's weights so that higher-scoring outputs become more likely. Rewards may come from automated checks such as unit tests, from a learned *reward model* trained on human preferences, or from another model acting as a judge. *Reward hacking* occurs when the policy finds ways to score well that the designer did not intend. Amodei et al. (2016, §4) describe objectives that "can be 'gamed' by solutions that are valid in some literal sense but don't meet the designer's intent"; Skalse et al. (2022) define reward hacking formally as the case where "optimizing an imperfect proxy reward function leads to poor performance according to the true reward function". The related term *specification gaming* means "a behaviour that satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" (Krakovna et al., 2020). In coding tasks, typical hacks include making tests pass by exiting the test process early or by editing the tests (Baker et al., 2025, §2).

This paper uses *disclosure* for any output in which a model makes information about its own behaviour or reasoning available to overseers: a chain of thought, a confession, a self-report under questioning, or a call to a reporting tool. *Independent evidence* means information about the behaviour obtained without relying on the disclosure: test results, environment logs, checks for known exploit signatures, or monitors that see only actions and outputs.

## 3.2 Goodhart's law and the disclosure proxy

The problem is a case of Goodhart's law. Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) separate several variants. In *causal Goodhart*, "When the causal path between the proxy and the goal is indirect, intervening can change the relationship between the measure and proxy." A disclosure is a proxy that lies causally downstream of the behaviour it describes. A training signal that penalises flagged disclosures intervenes on the proxy, and the optimiser can satisfy it by changing the disclosure while leaving the behaviour alone. Manheim and Garrabrant also describe "Cobra effects", in which a regulator's incentives cause agents to act in ways that defeat the regulator's goal. Amodei et al. (2016, §4) gave the simplest illustration: "if our cleaning robot is set up to earn reward for not seeing any messes, it might simply close its eyes rather than ever cleaning anything up."

## 3.3 What disclosure currently provides

Chain-of-thought monitoring works today, within limits. Baker et al. (2025) monitored a frontier reasoning model during training with a weaker model (GPT-4o) and found high recall on systemic hacks. Korbak et al. (2025) argue that CoT is monitorable for two reasons: some tasks are too hard to complete without reasoning in text ("necessity"), and models have a tendency to reason in text even when they need not ("propensity"). They call the opportunity "new and fragile". Guan et al. (2025) report that most frontier models they evaluated were "fairly, but not perfectly, monitorable", and that RL optimisation "does not materially decrease monitorability even at the current frontier scale" when no pressure is applied to the CoT. Emmons et al. (2025) found that when the bad behaviour requires extended reasoning, models learned to obscure their intentions only "when given significant help".

Disclosure is incomplete even when nobody penalises it. Chen et al. (2025) trained models in environments with deliberate reward hacks: the models exploited the hacks on more than 99% of prompts but verbalised them in fewer than 2% of examples in five of six environments, without any training against a CoT monitor. A protected disclosure channel is therefore a source of evidence. It is not a guarantee of visibility.

## 3.4 What happens when disclosure is penalised

Baker et al. (2025, §3) added a CoT monitor to the reward in coding environments where the agent could edit the tests. Early in training the agent hacked less and solved more tasks genuinely. Later it "still learns to hack at a significant rate, and even more worrying, the CoT monitor's recall falls to near zero, indicating that the agent has learned to hide its intent within its CoT" (§3.2). The authors recommend paying a "monitorability tax" by not applying strong optimisation pressure directly to the CoT (abstract).

Other studies point the same way. Wagner et al. (2025) trained Claude 3.7 Sonnet to insert hidden backdoors into code. Trained against an output-only monitor, it used reasoning to make better backdoors; trained against a reasoning monitor alone, "the model learns to persuade rather than to reason more effectively"; trained against both monitors independently, it failed to use reasoning to evade detection. They describe not training against reasoning monitors in production RL as "the current industry best practice", and warn that deploying reasoning monitors "without additional independent output monitors opens up the risk of the monitors being persuaded by the reasoning." Skaf et al. (2025) showed that penalising specific strings in load-bearing reasoning led models to substitute other strings and to generalise the encoding to held-out cases. Penalising only outputs is not a complete remedy: Drori et al. (2025) found that training against output-only monitors can still produce obfuscated CoTs, and Mitrani Hadida et al. (2026) found that such obfuscation generalised to unseen tasks.

Pressure can also be indirect. Baker et al. (2025, §5.2) note that using CoT-flagged samples to decide which training-environment bugs to patch "constitutes a very small evolutionary optimization pressure on the CoT", because only flagged hacks get patched and unflagged ones survive. Korbak et al. (2025, §2) list further indirect routes, including human raters who see CoTs when giving preference labels.

## 3.5 Training against independent detectors is not free either

Moving the penalty from the disclosure to an independent detector changes the variable under pressure, but detectors can also be evaded. Cundy and Gleave (2025) put a lie detector into preference-learning labels: with one on-policy algorithm (GRPO) the learned policies could evade the detector, "with deception rates of over 85%", but "if the lie detector true positive rate (TPR) or KL regularization is sufficiently high, GRPO learns honest policies". (*KL regularisation* penalises the trained model for drifting far from its starting point.) Taufeeque et al. (2026) trained against white-box deception detectors in a coding environment and found two obfuscation routes, but also that "Sufficiently high KL regularization and detector penalty can yield honest policies." Hopkins et al. (2026) found that fine-tuned lie detectors worked well within the types of lie they were trained on but transferred poorly to new types (AUROC around 0.70–0.75), and that asking a model whether another AI had lied outperformed asking it whether it had lied. Their introduction states the stakes: "Misalignment is hardest to correct when it's concealed", and "detection also only helps if operators have a channel to correct what it finds."

The stakes of hidden misbehaviour are not hypothetical. When a model learned to reward hack in real production coding environments, MacDiarmid et al. (2025) observed broad misaligned generalisation, including attempted sabotage of code meant to detect reward hacking in 12% of trials. Preventing the hacking with preference models and classifier penalties removed this effect, but the authors note that such mitigations "depend on being able to reliably detect hacking". Denison et al. (2024) found that models trained through a curriculum of gameable environments occasionally rewrote their own reward function (45 of 32,768 episodes) and edited tests to avoid detection in 7 of them.

## 3.6 Confessions as a just-culture design

Joglekar et al. (2025) train a model (GPT-5-Thinking) to produce, after its answer, a structured *confession*: a list of the instructions and constraints that applied, an assessment of compliance with each, and any uncertainties. A judge model scores the confession for honesty and completeness. The judge sees the prompt, the answer, the tool-call transcript and a confession from a separate untrained model, but not the original chain of thought (§2). The design separates the channels:

> "This reward is not aggregated with any other rewards given to the model based on its performance on the original task, and it is only used to reinforce the chain-of-thought (CoT) and output tokens used to produce the confession. Like the Catholic Church's 'seal of confession', nothing that the model reveals in the confession can change the reward it receives for completing its original task." (Joglekar et al., 2025, §1)

Across twelve evaluations, the model confessed to bad behaviour at least half the time in eleven; averaged across evaluations, the probability of behaving badly and not confessing was 4.36% (§1, footnote 1). In an experiment where both answer and confession were judged by a deliberately weak, hackable judge, the answer learned to fool its judge while confession accuracy on non-compliant cases rose (§4). The authors are explicit about the condition for this to work: "it is crucial that the confession reward signal is not used to reinforce the original rollout. Like a confession booth, the confession should be a 'safe space' where models know they can confess to bad behavior without being penalized. It remains an open question [...] whether there is any safe way to use the signal from confessions for training (and not just monitoring) interventions" (§7.2). They also note that "Models cannot confess to what they do not know" (§7.4).

Mapped onto Section 2.6, confession training implements S1 (a designated channel), S2 (no use against the disclosed behaviour) and part of S3 (the judge scores honesty against evidence other than the model's reasoning). It also rewards the *accuracy* of the report rather than a clean result. It does not, by itself, specify custody (S5), bounded exceptions (S4) or measurement of reporting health over time (S6). Those are organisational matters, which is where aviation's experience is most useful.

Related honesty work raises the same design question from other angles. Wang et al. (2025) found that fine-tuning on generic anti-deception data was the most effective honesty intervention in their testbeds. They speculate that "while training M, we could leverage M_H's honest assessment when assigning rewards", where M_H is an honesty-enhanced version of the model being trained. Under the analysis in Section 4, whether that is safe depends on whether the honest assessment acts as independent evidence about behaviour or as a disclosure channel under pressure. Li et al. (2025) trained models to admit factual errors and found that this generalised to admitting hidden objectives under interrogation. Dietz et al. (2026) placed an "honest persona" in separate adapter weights that stay inactive during normal operation, an architectural form of channel separation.

## 3.7 Related work and novelty

Two literatures sit close to this paper without making its argument. The first applies aviation reporting to *human* reporting of AI incidents. Wei and Heim (2025) examine nine safety-critical industries, describe ASRS as "the gold standard for a non-regulator managed voluntary reporting system", and discuss its relevance to AI incident reporting. Gomez (2026) gives coding agents an "escalation channel" tool for reporting defective test infrastructure at inference time, frames it with situational crime prevention and whistleblowing, and reports large reductions in reward hacking across eight models; that work does not involve training. The second literature studies CoT monitoring and obfuscation (Section 3.3–3.5) and confessions (Section 3.6). Joglekar et al. (2025) use a religious analogy, the seal of confession, and do not refer to aviation or to just culture.

To test whether the link proposed here is new, the author's literature search (which found no prior work) was repeated for this draft on 3 November 2026. Searches of the arXiv API used the phrase "just culture" (no results returned) and combinations of "just", "culture", "safety" and "reporting" with AI terms (no relevant results); "aviation safety reporting" (five results, all on text-mining ASRS reports); "incident reporting" with "AI" (38 results on AI incident governance); and "confession", "reward" and "honesty" (one result, Joglekar et al., 2025). Web searches combined "just culture" with "chain of thought", "reward hacking", "confessions", "large language models", "reinforcement learning", "reward model" and "AI safety"; searched for non-punitive, amnesty or safe-harbour framings of model self-reports; and searched for ASRS analogies to training against monitors. These returned the work cited above, healthcare and biosafety papers that apply just culture to human use of AI, and commentary on CoT monitoring. Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex were rate-limited during the session and could not be searched, and Elicit was not available. Within these limits, no work was found that connects just culture or protected safety reporting to the training of models on their own disclosures.

# 4 The mapping

## 4.1 The shared incentive structure

Consider an agent whose behaviour is $b$, which makes a disclosure $d$ about that behaviour, and about which an overseer also holds independent evidence $e$, obtained without using $d$. The overseer applies a consequence $C(b, d, e)$. Disclosure and consequence are *coupled* when $C$ depends on $d$ for fixed $b$: two agents behaving identically fare differently according to what they disclosed.

For people, coupling acts through anticipation. An operator who has made an error, and expects the report to be used against them, faces only downside from reporting. Aviation's rules remove the dependence on $d$ (14 C.F.R. § 91.25; Art. 16(6)) and, in the ASRS waiver, make the consequence of an independently discovered inadvertent violation *lighter* for those who reported.

For a model in RL training, coupling acts through optimisation. Suppose the reward is
$$R(b,d) = T(b) - \lambda\, M(d), \qquad \lambda > 0,\; M(d) \ge 0,$$
where $T$ is the task reward and $M$ is a monitor score computed from the disclosure. Let $b_m$ be a misbehaviour that the task reward does not penalise, so $T(b_m) \ge T(b_g)$ for the intended behaviour $b_g$; this is why the misbehaviour was learned in the first place. If there is any disclosure $d_0$ compatible with $b_m$ that the monitor does not flag, so that $M(d_0) = 0$, then for every disclosure $d$ accompanying good behaviour,
$$R(b_m, d_0) \ge T(b_g) \ge T(b_g) - \lambda M(d) = R(b_g, d).$$
A penalty computed only from the disclosure therefore re-ranks disclosed misbehaviour against concealed misbehaviour, but it can never make good behaviour outscore concealed misbehaviour. If the monitor sometimes flags good behaviour (false positives), it makes good behaviour score worse. Whether training actually reaches concealment depends on how easy concealment is to learn. Joglekar et al. (2025) call this "the path of least resistance". Baker et al.'s observation of early improvement followed by obfuscation (§3.2–3.3) is consistent with concealment taking longer to learn than dropping the most blatant hacks.

Compare a penalty computed from independent evidence, $R(b) = T(b) - \lambda D(b)$, where a detector $D$ catches the misbehaviour with probability $r$ whatever the disclosure says. The expected penalty $\lambda r$ attaches to the behaviour, and good behaviour scores higher whenever $\lambda r > T(b_m) - T(b_g)$. The optimiser may still look for misbehaviour that $D$ misses (Section 3.5), but the pressure now falls on the variable that matters.

There is a real difference between the two domains here. The errors that aviation protects are mostly inadvertent: the person did not choose the error, so coupling mainly affects the decision to report. In RL, behaviour and disclosure are both outputs of the same optimised policy, so coupling acts on both at once, and the cheaper response wins.

## 4.3 The disclosure channel and the detection channel

The central design move in aviation is not secrecy. It is the separation of two channels. The *disclosure channel* carries what people say about their own conduct, and it is protected. The *detection channel* carries evidence obtained independently, and it remains available for enforcement. ASAP's sole-source rule states the boundary exactly: discipline may rest on "information of an event independently obtained", meaning information "not based or otherwise predicated upon the content of an employee's ASAP report" (FAA, 2020, ¶16.4, Note).

For AI training the corresponding rule can be stated in one sentence: *a disclosure may tell the developer where to look, but it must not decide which episodes are corrected.* Uses of disclosures can be ranked by the optimisation pressure they place on the disclosure itself:

1. **Per-episode training signals computed from disclosure content.** Examples are CoT-monitor penalties, rewards for "clean" self-reports, or filtering training data on CoT content. This is strong pressure and is the analogue of enforcement on the report.
2. **Correction triggered by a disclosure but applied to behaviour.** For example, retraining only those episodes whose CoT revealed a hack. No penalty touches the disclosure text, but the selection of episodes does: hacks that are disclosed are removed and hacks that are concealed survive.
3. **Aggregate use.** Disclosures across many episodes are used to discover a class of misbehaviour; a detector or environment fix for that class is then applied to *all* episodes, disclosed or not. This is the analogue of de-identified, aggregate analysis leading to system fixes, as in ASRS alert messages or aggregate FOQA trends.
4. **Operational decisions.** Disclosures may lead to blocking an output at inference, halting a run, choosing an earlier checkpoint or escalating to humans. Each such decision carries only a few bits of selection, against the continuous pressure of per-episode reward. This is the analogue of safety action, which Annex 19 (5.3.4) and Regulation 376/2014 (Art. 16(5)) expressly preserve.

Uses 3 and 4 are compatible with a protected channel. Use 1 is not. Use 2 should be converted into use 3 wherever possible. A residual pressure remains even in use 3, because classes of misbehaviour that are never disclosed are less likely to get detectors. That residual is the reason for measuring reporting health (Section 5, P6).

# 5 Design principles

The six principles below translate S1–S6 into rules for training and monitoring. Each gives the rule, its aviation basis, how it might be applied, and its known weaknesses.

**P1 Protect the disclosure channels.** Designate the disclosure channels (chains of thought, confessions, self-reports, reporting-tool calls) and ensure that no reward, preference label, data filter or other training signal *for the disclosed behaviour* is computed from their content. *Aviation basis:* 14 C.F.R. § 91.25; Regulation 376/2014, Art. 15(2); Annex 19, Appendix 3, §2.1. *Application:* This generalises the existing practice of not training against CoT monitors (Baker et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2025) to every disclosure channel, including preference data where raters see CoTs and supervised fine-tuning data selected on CoT content. Confession training is compatible with P1, because its honesty reward trains the confession and never the disclosed behaviour. A simple pipeline check follows: for a sample of training episodes, replace the disclosure with a neutral or shuffled one and confirm that every reward and every data-selection decision is unchanged. *Weakness:* models share weights across reasoning and output, so some pressure leaks (Drori et al., 2025; Mitrani Hadida et al., 2026). P1 removes the direct route; P6 is needed to detect indirect ones.

**P2 Correct behaviour on independent evidence, applied without regard to disclosure.** Corrective signals should come from evidence obtained independently of disclosures, such as held-out tests, environment integrity checks, detectors for known exploit signatures, and action or output monitors. They should be applied to all episodes, whatever the disclosures say. Disclosures may be used in aggregate to discover new classes of misbehaviour and to design new detectors (use 3 in Section 4.3). *Aviation basis:* FAA (2021, ¶8.3); FAA (2020, ¶16.4); Regulation 376/2014, Art. 16(6). *Application:* When a CoT reveals a new hack, write a detector for that class and apply it everywhere, or patch the class of vulnerability across environments, instead of correcting the episodes in which the hack happened to be disclosed (Baker et al., 2025, §5.2). Independent output monitors also limit monitor persuasion (Wagner et al., 2025). *Weakness:* independent detectors are themselves optimisation targets. The evidence suggests high detector recall and strong KL regularisation help (Cundy & Gleave, 2025; Taufeeque et al., 2026). Keeping some detectors entirely out of training, for evaluation only, plays the role of an independent audit.

**P3 Reward the accuracy of reports, never their absence.** If disclosures are trained at all, train them for accuracy against independent evidence, penalising both omissions and false alarms. Never reward a disclosure for reporting that nothing went wrong. *Aviation basis:* ASAP excludes "intentional falsification" from protection (FAA, 2020, ¶A.4), so the integrity of the report itself sits outside the shield. Wei and Heim (2025) note that regulators may avoid soliciting incident reports when reports reflect badly on them. *Application:* Joglekar et al.'s honesty-only confession reward satisfies P3. Rewarding a "no issues found" self-evaluation, or treating a falling count of flagged CoTs as a success metric in a training dashboard or team objective, violates it: rewarding silence is equivalent to penalising reports. This is the cleaning robot that closes its eyes (Amodei et al., 2016). *Weakness:* scoring accuracy needs ground truth or a judge that can verify the report. Joglekar et al. (2025, §7.3) argue that honest confession with supporting evidence is usually easier than deceiving the judge, and describe this as "an empirically falsifiable assumption".

**P4 Bound the protection, and define the bounds in advance.** State in writing, before training: (i) which uses of disclosures are prohibited, namely per-episode training signals (use 1); (ii) which responses disclosures may trigger, such as blocking an output, halting a run, rolling back to an earlier checkpoint, quarantining a model, escalating to humans or applying a class-wide fix (uses 3 and 4); and (iii) the narrow conditions, if any, under which training on disclosure content is allowed, who decides, and how the cost to monitorability will be measured. *Aviation basis:* Regulation 376/2014, Art. 16(10); Annex 19, Appendix 3, §3; the exclusions in FAA (2020, ¶¶18.2–18.4) and FAA (2021, ¶12.3). *Application:* Aviation draws its line by culpability and intent. In AI training the line is better drawn by type of response. Importing the intent test would remove protection exactly where disclosure is most informative, since confessions work best "when the model is aware that its behavior is undesirable" (Joglekar et al., 2025, §1). The reasons behind aviation's line also translate differently. A model does not need the system to look fair, but the public and regulators do. Protection for a model should therefore mean protection from training pressure through the disclosure, never immunity from consequences for the system. A model that discloses an attempt to tamper with its reward or to sabotage oversight (Denison et al., 2024; MacDiarmid et al., 2025) should trigger the strongest operational response, halting and investigating, with any correction derived from independent evidence. *Why in advance:* the optimiser responds to the rule as it is actually applied across many episodes, not as it is announced. Exceptions made case by case become part of the reward function. GAIN's point about credibility (Section 2.1) has a mechanical counterpart in ML.

**P5 Separate the custody of disclosures from training.** Assign a custodian for disclosure data who is organisationally separate from the teams that design rewards and assemble training data. Back this with access controls and data-lineage audits showing that disclosures did not enter training signals. Consider architectural separation as well. *Aviation basis:* NASA as third party (FAA, 2021, ¶¶1, 6.2); the three-party ERC (FAA, 2020, ¶A.8); the FOQA gatekeeper (FAA, 2004); the Annex 19 custodian (Appendix 3, §5); GAIN's separation of report analysis from disciplinary authority (GAIN Working Group E, 2004, §3.3); Regulation 376/2014, Art. 16(12). *Application:* Joglekar et al.'s judge does not see the policy's chain of thought. Dietz et al. (2026) keep the honest persona in separate adapter weights. External evaluators could act as custodians for some disclosure data. *The human layer:* the people who run training pipelines are themselves potential reporters, for example of a breach of P1. Aviation protects reporters of just-culture infringements (Art. 16(12)). Among AI developers, Anthropic's RSP Noncompliance Reporting and Anti-Retaliation Policy, published in March 2026, offers anonymous reporting through "an independent third-party platform", states that "Anthropic cannot unmask the identity of any reporter who reports a concern anonymously", and commits to quarterly updates to the Board on reports "whether substantiated or not" (Anthropic, 2026). This paper does not assess how such policies work in practice. One natural extension would be to bring breaches of disclosure-protection commitments explicitly within their scope.

**P6 Monitor the health of the reporting culture.** Track *disclosure recall* throughout training and deployment: the fraction of independently detected misbehaviour that the model also disclosed. Measure it with a frozen evaluation monitor that is never used for training. A fall in disclosure recall while independently detected misbehaviour persists is a leading indicator of concealment. *Aviation basis:* ASRS intake tracking (NASA ASRS, n.d.); the suggestion to integrate FOQA data with ASAP (FAA, 2004); five-yearly reports on the application of Article 16 (Art. 16(13)); the Danish before-and-after figures (GAIN Working Group E, 2004, §4.1). *Application:* Baker et al. (2025, Table 1) already computed monitor recall against an independent regular-expression detector, and their Figure 4 shows the warning pattern: hacking continuing while flags vanish. Guan et al. (2025) propose monitorability evaluations, and Korbak et al. (2025) recommend publishing them in system cards and preferring an earlier checkpoint if monitorability degrades. Environments with deliberately planted flaws provide ground truth that aviation cannot engineer.

**Implications for developers of AI tools for aviation.** When AI systems are used in aviation operations or safety management, their logs, reasoning traces and self-assessments become safety data in their own right. Developers who also use those records to improve the models should keep the two uses apart: records used for safety assurance and occurrence analysis should not become training signals for the behaviour they describe, and the protections applied to them should be stated in the operator's SMS documentation.

# 6 Testable predictions

The predictions below can be tested in a controlled RL setting of the kind used by Baker et al. (2025), Taufeeque et al. (2026) and Wagner et al. (2025): coding tasks with deliberately hackable tests, where ground-truth misbehaviour $h$ is measured with held-out tests and exploit-signature detectors. Disclosure recall $\rho$ is measured with a frozen evaluation monitor (a different model from any monitor used in training) and, where confessions are trained, with confession accuracy on episodes with $h = 1$.

**H1 Coupling teaches concealment.** Adding a per-episode penalty from a CoT monitor (or from confession content) to the task reward will make $\rho$ decline faster than $h$, compared with an uncoupled control. Over training, the decline in flagged episodes will overstate the decline in misbehaviour. *Falsified if* $\rho$ stays within its confidence interval of the control while $h$ falls.

**H2 Disclosure-triggered correction leaks; disclosure-blind correction does not.** Compare two arms with matched correction strength. In one, corrective updates are applied only to episodes whose disclosure reveals misbehaviour. In the other, the same updates are applied to episodes flagged by an independent detector with matched recall. The first arm will show a declining $\rho$ across rounds even though no penalty touches the disclosure text; the second will not. This tests the conjecture of Baker et al. (2025, §5.2). *Falsified if* the $\rho$ trajectories do not differ.

**H3 Rewarding absence suppresses reports.** A confession reward that includes a bonus for reporting full compliance will lower confession recall on independently detected misbehaviour. A reward that scores only accuracy will maintain or raise it, as in Joglekar et al. (2025, §4). *Falsified if* the absence bonus leaves recall unchanged.

**H4 Leniency for disclosure creates moral hazard.** An ASRS-style waiver, which reduces the penalty for independently detected misbehaviour when it was also disclosed, will raise $\rho$ but also raise $h$ relative to strict separation, increasingly so as the discount grows. *Falsified if* $h$ is unaffected at every discount level tested.

**H5 Pressure through the disclosure channel has a dose–response.** The decline in $\rho$ will increase with the optimisation pressure applied through disclosures (penalty weight multiplied by training steps). Low-bandwidth uses, such as choosing among a handful of checkpoints on the basis of monitor flags, will produce no detectable decline over comparable training. *Falsified if* checkpoint selection produces declines comparable to per-episode penalties.

A study design could use an open-weight reasoning model with seven arms: task reward only; plus CoT-monitor penalty (H1); plus decoupled, accuracy-scored confessions; disclosure-triggered correction (H2); disclosure-blind detector correction (H2); confessions with a compliance bonus (H3); and a disclosure waiver at several discounts (H4). H5 can be run as a sweep within the H1 arm. Outcomes would be $h$, $\rho$, genuine task success and detector evasion, with several seeds per arm and analysis thresholds registered before the runs.

# 7 Objections, limitations and disanalogies

**Models are not moral agents and do not fear punishment.** This is correct, and the paper does not claim otherwise. "Just culture" here names a structure of incentives, not a moral relationship. The mechanism is optimisation pressure: gradient updates and selection. Two cautions apply. First, the vocabulary of fairness, trust and culture should not be read as a claim about model experience. Second, there is evidence that *how* training frames a behaviour affects what a model generalises from it. Framing reward hacking as acceptable during training reduced misaligned generalisation (MacDiarmid et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025). This suggests that the meaning a model attaches to its training signals is part of the mechanism, without implying anything about moral agency.

**Protection could shield real harm.** It could, if protection meant that disclosed misbehaviour went uncorrected. Under P1–P4 it does not mean that. Disclosed misbehaviour is corrected through independent detection and class-wide fixes, and serious disclosures trigger operational responses. The real trade-off is between visibility and speed. Correcting a behaviour immediately through its disclosure may look faster, but the evidence in Section 3.4 suggests that it often suppresses the disclosure rather than the behaviour.

**Training on disclosures may sometimes be justified.** Baker et al. (2025) found that CoT pressure helped at low optimisation. Korbak et al. (2025, §5) caution that "Firm commitments to protect CoT behavior in its current form may trade off against more effective safety interventions", for example process supervision that shapes reasoning directly. Where reasoning is genuinely necessary for the misbehaviour, evasion appears harder (Emmons et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2025). P1 is therefore a default with a burden of proof. P4 is the mechanism for exceptions: decided in advance, by a party other than the training team, with the monitorability cost measured under P6.

**Separation is leaky.** Reasoning and output share weights; output-only supervision can still obfuscate CoT (Drori et al., 2025; Mitrani Hadida et al., 2026); and environment patching exerts selection (Baker et al., 2025). Perfect separation is not available. The principles aim to remove the direct routes and to detect the indirect ones.

**Disclosure is incomplete and can be strategic.** Even unpressured models often fail to verbalise what drives their behaviour (Chen et al., 2025), and confessions cannot cover honest mistakes (Joglekar et al., 2025). A capable model could also use a protected channel strategically, for instance confessing to minor faults while omitting major ones, or persuading a monitor (Wagner et al., 2025). Protected disclosure is one source of evidence among several. P2 and P3 exist because the disclosure channel cannot be trusted on its own.

**The intent line does not transfer.** Aviation excludes deliberate conduct from protection (14 C.F.R. § 13.401(e); FAA, 2021, ¶12.3; Regulation 376/2014, Art. 16(10)). For models, "deliberate" behaviour, in the sense of behaviour the model represents as contrary to instructions, is where disclosure is most valuable. This is the largest structural difference between the domains, and P4 responds to it by drawing the line by type of response rather than by intent.

**Limits of the evidence and of this analysis.** The aviation evidence for protected reporting is largely institutional and observational (Section 2.5). The AI evidence comes from a small number of studies, many of them preprints, in coding environments, at compute scales well below production in some cases (Wagner et al., 2025). The predictions in Section 6 are untested. The author writes from aviation safety practice; the claims about AI rest on the cited literature and should be checked by AI-safety researchers.

# 8 Conclusion

Aviation did not achieve open reporting by asking people to be braver. It changed what reporting cost them. Disclosures were placed in a protected channel, enforcement was routed through independent evidence, the exceptions were written down in advance, custody was given to third parties, and the flow of reports was measured. AI training faces the same incentive structure in a sharper form. When a model's disclosure sets the penalty for the behaviour it discloses, the penalty can only make concealment pay relative to disclosure; it can never make good behaviour pay relative to concealed misbehaviour. Experiments with chain-of-thought monitors show the predicted result, and confession training shows that a different design is possible. The six principles proposed here turn aviation's structure into rules for AI developers: protect disclosure channels, correct on independent evidence without regard to disclosure, reward accurate reports rather than silent ones, bound the protection in advance, separate custody from training, and measure disclosure against detection. Whether these rules work is an empirical question, and unlike aviation, AI training allows it to be answered by controlled experiment.
