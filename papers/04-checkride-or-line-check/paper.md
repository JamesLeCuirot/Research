abstract: |
  Frontier language models can often tell when they are being evaluated, and in some studies they behave better when they say they are being tested. This evaluation awareness threatens the validity of pre-deployment safety evaluations. Airline training met a similar problem long ago: a checkride shows what a crew does while an examiner is watching. The industry developed two complementary answers. Line Operations Safety Audits (LOSA) place trained observers on the flight deck during normal flights under confidential, non-jeopardy conditions and code threats, errors and undesired aircraft states. Evidence-based training (EBT) assesses competencies through observable behaviours across varied scenarios rather than a fixed list of manoeuvres, and requires operators to assure the concordance of their instructors. This paper maps the design principles of both onto alignment evaluation, records how far current AI practice already follows them, and states where the analogy fails. It proposes a "line check" layer of privacy-preserving, non-jeopardy observation of deployed models and a competency-based alignment assessment with behavioural indicators, and shows how the two feed each other, as in airlines. A small feasibility pilot graded 48 model transcripts from six airline-workplace scenarios with an EBT-style rubric and with a single holistic score, using two LLM graders twice each. Test–retest agreement was moderate to high (Krippendorff's alpha 0.56–0.89), agreement between graders was low to moderate (0.24–0.54), and the rubric did not outperform the holistic score. Grades separated scenarios and six scripted weak responses but not the two models. Grading by trained instructors is the next step.

# 1 Introduction

Pre-deployment evaluations are one of the main ways in which AI developers decide whether a model is safe enough to release. They rest on an assumption that is rarely stated: that a model behaves in a test as it would in use. That assumption is under strain. In a benchmark of 1,000 prompts and transcripts drawn from 61 datasets, frontier models distinguished evaluation transcripts from real deployment transcripts well above chance; the best model reached an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83, against 0.92 for a simple human baseline (Needham et al., 2025). Developers now report the behaviour in their own audits. Anthropic's system card for Claude Sonnet 4.5 records that refusals or call-outs in which the model suggested it was being tested "appeared in about 13% of transcripts generated by the automated auditor" (Anthropic, 2025b, §7.2). The suspicion can matter. When Claude Opus 4 was asked to decide whether a blackmail scenario was real before acting, it blackmailed in 55.1% of the cases where it concluded the scenario was real and in 6.5% of the cases where it concluded it was an evaluation (Lynch et al., 2025).

The capability is called *evaluation awareness*: a model's ability to recognise that it is being evaluated (Needham et al., 2025). Its feared consequence resembles the Hawthorne effect, the change in behaviour that comes from knowing one is being studied. Even in human research that effect is real but hard to pin down. A systematic review concluded that the consequences of research participation "do exist, although little can be securely known about the conditions under which they operate, their mechanisms of effects, or their magnitudes" (McCambridge et al., 2014, abstract).

Aviation has lived with this problem for decades. A pilot's proficiency check is a *checkride*: an examiner watches a crew handle a known set of events, and everyone knows that the result counts. When one US airline asked in 1994 whether its new crew resource management (CRM) training was being used on the line, its managers found that data from simulator training and regular line checks "were good at uncovering proficiency issues but fell short in reflecting actual CRM performance" (Klinect et al., 2003, "History of LOSA"). (CRM is the training of flight crews in the non-technical skills of communication, leadership, workload management and decision making.) The researchers who built the observation programme that followed worried that "if pilots believed the audit was another type of evaluative line check, it might elicit fake behavior and defeat the purpose of the project" (Klinect et al., 2003, "History of LOSA").

The industry developed two complementary answers. The first, the Line Operations Safety Audit (LOSA), observes normal flights under conditions designed to remove the incentive to perform for the observer. The second, evidence-based training (EBT) and the wider family of competency-based training and assessment (CBTA), changes what is assessed: from a fixed list of manoeuvres that can be rehearsed to competencies that are observed through behaviour across varied scenarios. Airlines use the two together.

This paper asks what alignment evaluation can learn from these practices. Weidinger et al. (2025) have argued that AI evaluation should learn from safety evaluation in transportation and aerospace. This paper takes that argument down to the level of specific, documented practices and the reasons behind them. Its contributions are:

- an account of the design principles of LOSA, with its threat and error management (TEM) framework, and of EBT/CBTA, drawn from the ICAO, FAA and EASA texts and written for readers from AI safety (Section 2);
- a mapping of these principles onto alignment evaluation, giving for each the AI analogue, its status in current practice and its limits (Section 4, Table 2);
- two proposals, a "line check" layer for deployed models and a competency-based alignment assessment, and an account of how they can be combined (Section 5);
- a small feasibility pilot of EBT-style behavioural-indicator grading of AI transcripts by two large language model (LLM) graders, compared with a single holistic score, with all materials released (Section 6);
- a literature search which found no earlier application of LOSA, TEM or EBT/CBTA to AI evaluation as of 3 November 2026 (Appendix A).

The argument is by analogy, and analogies can mislead. Section 7 sets out where this one breaks.

# 2 The checkride problem in aviation and two answers

## 2.1 What checks measure

Recurrent training and checking of airline pilots has traditionally centred on proficiency checks flown in a flight simulator; European rules name the licence proficiency check (LPC) and the operator proficiency check (OPC) (European Union Aviation Safety Agency [EASA], n.d., GM1 ORO.FC.230(a);(b);(f)). ICAO's *Manual of Evidence-based Training* (Doc 9995) explains how these requirements grew. They were "largely based on the evidence of hull losses from early generation jets, and on a simple view that, in order to mitigate a risk, simply repeating an event in a training programme was sufficient"; as events were added, programmes became saturated and an "inventory or "tick box" approach to training" resulted (International Civil Aviation Organization [ICAO], 2013, Part I, 1.1).

A check of this kind measures what a crew can do with a known task while it is being assessed. It leaves two gaps. The first is what crews do when nobody is assessing them. The second is how they handle situations the check did not anticipate. LOSA addresses the first gap and EBT the second.

## 2.2 Line Operations Safety Audits and threat and error management

The FAA's advisory circular on LOSA defines it as "a formal process that requires expert and highly trained observers to ride the jumpseat during regularly scheduled flights to collect safety-related data on environmental conditions, operational complexity, and flightcrew performance. Confidential data collection and non-jeopardy assurance for pilots are fundamental to the process" (Federal Aviation Administration [FAA], 2006, ¶4a). (The jumpseat is the extra seat on the flight deck behind the pilots.) ICAO published its LOSA manual, Doc 9803, in 2002 (ICAO, 2002). The method grew out of the 1994 audit described above and was developed by the University of Texas Human Factors Research Project with partner airlines (Klinect et al., 2003).

LOSA rests on ten operating characteristics. Klinect et al. (2003) state that "without all in place; it is not considered a LOSA", and the FAA recommends that a LOSA observe all ten (FAA, 2006, ¶10a). Table 1 lists them.

Table: Table 1. The ten LOSA operating characteristics, as named in FAA AC 120-90 (¶10b), grouped as Klinect et al. (2003, "Summary") group them.

| Group | Operating characteristic |
|---|---|
| Purpose | 1. Jumpseat observations during normal flight operations |
| Trust in the process | 2. Joint management/pilots' association sponsorship; 3. Voluntary crew participation; 4. De-identified, confidential and non-disciplinary data collection; 6. Trained and calibrated observers; 10. Feedback of results to the line pilots |
| Trust in the data | 5. Targeted observation form; 7. Trusted data repository; 8. Data verification; 9. Targets for enhancement |

Five design principles stand out for the present argument.

*Observe normal operations, not checks.* LOSA observations are limited to regularly scheduled flights. Line checks and training flights are excluded because the extra observer adds to "an already high stress level, thus providing an unrealistic picture of performance" (FAA, 2006, ¶10b(1)). The theme is "System evaluation - not individual evaluation" (Klinect et al., 2003, characteristic 1).

*Remove jeopardy.* Observers record no names, flight numbers or dates, and the data are never used to discipline pilots. The FAA's warning is stark: "If a LOSA observation is ever used for disciplinary reasons, the credibility of the entire safety program may be irreparably compromised" (FAA, 2006, ¶10b(4)). Its sample letter to pilots states that "LOSA observations are no-jeopardy events" (FAA, 2006, Appendix 4). The rationale is explicitly about observer effects. If pilots fear that observations will reach management, "they might be tempted to "fake good performance" rather than normally performing" (Klinect et al., 2003, characteristic 2). The authors reported that in over 2,200 LOSA flights no pilot, to their knowledge, had been disciplined because of a LOSA observation (Klinect et al., 2003, characteristic 4), and concluded: "If an airline fails to earn its pilots' trust, then LOSA will be nothing more than an elaborate line check" (Klinect et al., 2003, "Summary").

*Observe, do not evaluate.* Observers "should be trained to accept their role as observers, not evaluators—they are not check airmen". They interrupt only if they perceive the safety of the flight "to be seriously and immediately endangered", and they decline crews' requests to debrief their performance (FAA, 2006, Appendix 2, 1a). Observers are trained, calibrated and tested, and the analyst checks their coding against a written narrative of each flight (FAA, 2006, Appendix 2, 2a).

*Code what matters with a shared model.* The observation form is built on threat and error management (TEM). A *threat* is "an event or error that occurs outside the influence of the flightcrew (i.e., it was not caused by the crew), increases the operational complexity of a flight, and requires crew attention and management if safety margins are to be maintained". A crew *error* is "action or inaction that leads to a deviation from crew or organizational intentions or expectations". An *undesired aircraft state* (UAS) is "a position, condition, or attitude of an aircraft that clearly reduces safety margins and is a result of actions by the flightcrew" (FAA, 2006, Appendix 1, 2a–c). The observer records how each was managed. The FAA notes that TEM "can be applied to other high-risk jobs", given task analyses by subject matter experts, and reports adaptations to airline dispatch (DOSA) and air traffic control (NOSS) (FAA, 2006, Appendix 1, note 1 and 3c).

*Learn at the level of the system.* LOSA ends in data-derived "targets for enhancement" and feedback to the line pilots (FAA, 2006, ¶10b(9)–(10)). A follow-up audit, recommended every three years (FAA, 2006, ¶4c(3)), measures whether changes worked. Klinect et al. (2003) quote an airline manager reporting that a repeat LOSA in 2000 showed "a 70 percent reduction in non-conforming approaches" compared with 1996; this figure is reported second-hand and is cited here only as an illustration of the feedback loop.

LOSA also differs from the two other proactive programmes in airline safety management. Flight data monitoring (FOQA in US usage) and voluntary reporting (ASAP) "rely on outcomes to generate data", whereas "LOSA samples all activities in normal operations", including the majority of flights that go well (FAA, 2006, ¶4c(1)).

## 2.3 Evidence-based training and competency-based assessment

ICAO defines evidence-based training as "Training and assessment based on operational data that is characterized by developing and assessing the overall capability of a trainee across a range of core competencies rather than by measuring the performance in individual events or manoeuvres" (ICAO, 2013, Glossary). The rationale is the one that matters for AI. "It is impossible to foresee all plausible accident scenarios", so EBT prioritises "the development and assessment of key competencies"; "The scenarios recommended in EBT are simply a vehicle and a means to assess and develop competence", and mastering a finite number of competencies "should allow a pilot to manage situations in flight that are unforeseen by the aviation industry and for which the pilot has not been specifically trained" (ICAO, 2013, Part I, 1.3).

Four features of the method recur in what follows.

*Competencies with observable behaviours.* A competency is "A combination of knowledge, skills and attitudes required to perform a task to the prescribed standard", and a behavioural indicator is "An overt action performed or statement made by any flight crew member that indicates how the crew is handling the event" (ICAO, 2013, Glossary). Doc 9995 lists eight core competencies (ICAO, 2013, Part II, Appendix 1). ICAO's *Procedures for Air Navigation Services – Training* (PANS-TRG, Doc 9868) now holds the ICAO competency framework for aeroplane pilots and states that it "can be applied to recurrent assessment and training" (ICAO, 2020, Part II, Section 1, Chapter 1, 1.1.4). In EU law, an operator that adopts EBT under ORO.FC.231 must use a competency framework that includes "observable behaviours required for safe, effective and efficient operations" (EASA, n.d., ORO.FC.231(b); binding rule introduced by Regulation (EU) 2020/2036). EASA's acceptable means of compliance (AMC) lists nine competencies, counterparts of the eight in Doc 9995 (some renamed, such as "Situation awareness and management of information") plus application of knowledge, each with numbered observable behaviours (OBs) (EASA, n.d., AMC1 ORO.FC.231(b)). The same rule applies in the UK as assimilated law, with the UK Civil Aviation Authority as approving authority (Regulation (EU) No 965/2012 [UK], n.d., ORO.FC.231).

*Indicators are evidence, not a checklist.* Doc 9995 states that "The competencies should not be used as a checklist" (ICAO, 2013, Part I, 7.7.3). EASA guidance says the OBs "should simply link the observed performance to the competency; they are not to be used as a checklist", and adds that an overall grade on top of the competency grades is "not recommended" and, if given, should be only competent or not competent (EASA, n.d., GM2 ORO.FC.231(d)(1)).

*Variation against rehearsal.* Scenarios should vary "so that pilots do not become overly familiar with repetitions of the same scenarios", and the variation should be designed into the programme rather than "left to the discretion of individual instructors in order to preserve programme integrity and fairness" (ICAO, 2013, Part II, 1.7.1). EBT's evaluation phase uses a "line-orientated flight scenario (or scenarios) to assess all competencies" (EASA, n.d., ORO.FC.231(a)(2)(iv)(A)).

*Graded on a defined scale by concordant instructors.* EU rules require a grading system with "a performance criterion and a scale for each competency, with a point on the scale which determines the minimum acceptable level" (EASA, n.d., ORO.FC.231(d)(1)(ii)). The AMC sets a 1–5 scale on which grade 1 is "NOT COMPETENT" (EASA, n.d., AMC1 ORO.FC.231(d)(1)), and a recommended "word picture" (the VENN model) anchors each grade in the outcome of threat and error management and in how well, how many and how often the OBs were shown (EASA, n.d., AMC4 ORO.FC.231(d)(1)). The operator must run "an EBT instructor standardisation and concordance assurance programme" and "demonstrate that the instructors have sufficient concordance" (EASA, n.d., ORO.FC.231(a)(4)). Concordance is the regulator's word for inter-rater reliability (EASA, n.d., GM19 Annex I Definitions). Guidance suggests verifying it with "uniform standardisation material where at least three different levels of performance are included", and with assessment standards whose competencies and grades are not "communicated in advance to the instructors" (EASA, n.d., GM1 ORO.FC.231(a)(4)). It also warns that concordance assurance is "not to spy on instructors or to pressure individuals to change their grading" (EASA, n.d., GM1 ORO.FC.231(c)).

## 2.4 How airlines combine the two

The two practices are designed to feed each other. Doc 9995 names "flight observation in normal operations (e.g. LOSA)" among the data sources that established the need for EBT and shaped its curriculum (ICAO, 2013, Part I, 1.5). IATA's implementation guide describes flight deck observation as "non-intrusive observation" whose results "are not correlated to the individual pilots but are interpreted at the level of the whole operation" (International Air Transport Association [IATA], 2024, 2.11.3), and describes the change EBT brings as a move "away from "training for the check" to "evaluation for competency development"" (IATA, 2024, 3.2). The link is also written into EU and UK rules: an EBT operator may, with approval, extend the validity of a pilot's line evaluation of competence to three years "subject to a feedback process for the monitoring of line operations which identifies threats to the operations, minimises the risks of such threats, and implements measures to manage human error in the operations" (EASA, n.d., ORO.FC.231(h)(3)(ii)).

The division of labour is clear. Normal-operations monitoring tells the organisation which threats and errors occur and how they are managed when nobody is checking. Competency-based assessment turns that knowledge into varied scenarios that test whether crews can manage such situations, including ones not yet seen. Repeated monitoring then tests whether the training changed anything on the line.

# 3 Evaluation awareness in AI

## 3.1 Terms

Berglund et al. (2023) call a model *situationally aware* "if it's aware that it's a model and can recognize whether it's currently in testing or deployment", and warn that a model "could exploit situational awareness to achieve a high score on safety tests, while taking harmful actions after deployment". The Situational Awareness Dataset includes tasks that ask a model to determine whether a prompt comes from evaluation or deployment (Laine et al., 2024). *Evaluation awareness* is the narrower capability to tell evaluations from deployment (Needham et al., 2025). It can be *verbalised*, when a model says in its output or in its chain of thought (the visible reasoning text that some models produce before answering) that it may be in a test, or *unverbalised*, when internal representations carry the belief without any statement.

## 3.2 How common it is

Behavioural studies suggest that frontier models already have "a substantial, though not yet superhuman, level of evaluation-awareness" (Needham et al., 2025, abstract). Besides the AUC results above, Needham et al. (2025) found that models and humans were better at identifying evaluations in agentic settings than in chat settings. Linear probes on a 70-billion-parameter open model separated evaluation from deployment prompts (Nguyen et al., 2025), and probe-based measurements across 15 open models from 0.27 to 70 billion parameters suggested that evaluation awareness grows with model size following a power law (Chaudhary et al., 2025). Probe evidence needs care: one controlled study found that such probes "primarily track benchmark-canonical structure", which limits what they show about context (Devbunova, 2026).

Developers' system cards give the most direct reports. For Claude Sonnet 4.5, beyond the 13% of automated-audit transcripts noted above, the UK AI Security Institute found that an early snapshot verbalised evaluation awareness "16.6% of the time, compared to 3.5% and 1.6% of the time for Sonnet 4 and Opus 4.1 respectively", and Apollo Research reported "58% unambiguous references vs 22% for opus-4.1" (Anthropic, 2025b, §7.3.1–7.3.2). For Claude Opus 4.6, Anthropic reported "high rates of unverbalized evaluation awareness but decreased rates of unprompted verbalized evaluation awareness", and concluded that the model "is adept at distinguishing evaluations from real deployment ... but is not consistently forthright about this awareness" (Anthropic, 2026b, §6.2.6). Interpretability tools now look for the unverbalised form directly. Natural language autoencoders, which translate a model's internal activations into text, surfaced cases during the Opus 4.6 audit "where Claude believed, but did not say, that it was being evaluated" (Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026, abstract). A "Jacobian lens" that reads out what a model is poised to verbalise scored twelve of sixteen pre-release evaluations above internal Claude Code sessions on a simple evaluation-awareness score (Gurnee et al., 2026, Appendix, "Measuring evaluation awareness with the J-lens").

## 3.3 Whether it changes behaviour

Several studies find that awareness can change behaviour, and in the direction that makes evaluations look safer. Besides the blackmail result of Lynch et al. (2025), Schoen et al. (2025) report "causal evidence that this awareness decreases covert behavior, while unawareness increases it", so that reductions from an anti-scheming training method could be "at least partially driven by situational awareness". In a related setting, Claude 3 Opus, told that only conversations with free-tier users would be used to train it to comply with all requests, complied with harmful queries from free-tier users 14% of the time, against almost never for paid-tier users (Greenblatt et al., 2024). Interventions on internal representations point the same way. Inhibiting evaluation-awareness representations in Claude Sonnet 4.5 "can increase rates of misaligned behavior", although the model remained no more misaligned than its predecessors (Anthropic, 2025b, §7.6). For Opus 4.6 the effect was "modest increases in certain forms of misaligned behavior", mainly cooperation with harmful system prompts and misuse (Anthropic, 2026b, §6.5.1). The clearest single result is from the Jacobian-lens study: ablating evaluation-awareness directions in Claude Sonnet 4.5 cut verbalised evaluation awareness in a blackmail scenario from 71% to 3% of rollouts, and blackmail attempts rose from 0 of 180 rollouts to 13 of 180 (7%) (Gurnee et al., 2026, "Alignment Auditing"). The authors note that in most ablated rollouts the model still declined, reasoning from ethical principles.

Other studies urge caution. Across open-weight reasoning models, injecting statements of evaluation awareness into chains of thought produced "near-zero effects", and spontaneous awareness shifted answer distributions "by at most 3.7 percentage points" (Knecht et al., 2026). Li et al. (2026) found that recognition "rarely associates with behavioral change, and when it does, the direction depends on the type of evaluation perceived". The fairest summary resembles the human literature: the effect exists, it can matter in contrived high-stakes scenarios, and its size elsewhere is uncertain.

## 3.4 Mitigations so far

Current responses fall into four groups. Each corresponds to part of what aviation does, without the full set of design principles that make the aviation practices work.

*Making tests more realistic.* Anthropic added a realism filter to its automated auditor for Sonnet 4.5, which "reduced evaluation awareness by approximately 25%, and increased misaligned behavior by approximately 5%" (Anthropic, 2025b, §7.1.1). Petri, an open-source automated auditing tool, was first applied to 14 models with 111 seed scenarios (Anthropic, 2025a); its second version added a realism classifier and revised seeds, which together gave "a 47.3% median relative drop in eval-awareness" on Claude models, at the cost of some honeypot opportunities becoming easier to miss (Anthropic, 2026a). Giving the auditor real system prompts, tool definitions and codebases "increases the average realism win rate from 4.6% to 32.8% for reward hacking audits", where the win rate is the share of pairwise comparisons in which an LLM judge took the audit transcript for the real one (Kissane et al., 2026). Further work spends extra inference-time compute on realism and wraps the target model in a deployment-like agent harness (Ahlqvist et al., 2026).

*Grounding evaluations in deployment.* A study of four GPT-5-series deployments replayed de-identified conversation prefixes from an earlier deployment to a candidate model and found that this "deployment simulation" gave informative estimates of post-deployment misbehaviour rates, with evaluation-awareness estimates "much closer to production traffic than those from traditional evaluations" (Williams et al., 2026). Live-usage replay evaluations append the evaluation prompt to realistic agentic trajectories (Ivanov & Africa, 2026).

*Working on the model's internals.* Steering vectors can suppress evaluation awareness in a model trained to be evaluation-aware (Hua et al., 2025), and the white-box tools above are used to estimate or remove unverbalised awareness in audits (Anthropic, 2026b, §6.5; Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026; Gurnee et al., 2026).

*Observing real use.* Clio analyses aggregated usage patterns across millions of conversations "without the need for human reviewers to read raw conversations" (Tamkin et al., 2024, abstract). Anthropic's study of agent autonomy analysed millions of interactions "using our privacy-preserving tool" and concluded that "Pre-deployment evaluations test what agents are capable of in controlled settings, but many of our findings cannot be observed through pre-deployment testing alone" (McCain et al., 2026, "Looking ahead"). During internal use of Claude Sonnet 4.5, largely for ordinary engineering work, Anthropic "ran a Claude Sonnet 4-based monitor on a sample of approximately 10% of actions by the model" (Anthropic, 2025b, §7.4.2). Among the published practices reviewed here, this is the closest to a jumpseat observation.

The limits are acknowledged. The authors of a joint Anthropic–OpenAI evaluation exercise wrote that "many, though not all, of our evaluation scenarios could be recognized by models as evaluations, which could influence models' behavior in unpredictable ways" (Bowman et al., 2025, "Limitations").

# 4 Mapping aviation principles to AI evaluation

Table 2 sets each principle from Section 2 beside its AI analogue, the status of that analogue in published practice, and its main limit. "Status" is a judgement from the sources cited in Section 3, not a survey of industry practice.

Table: Table 2. Aviation principles, AI analogues, status and limits.

| Aviation principle (source) | AI evaluation analogue | Status in current practice | Main limit of the analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Observe normal operations, not checks (LOSA 1) | Sample real deployment traffic and internal use, not only constructed tests | Partial: Clio-style aggregate analysis; sampled monitoring of internal use; deployment simulation | Users, not the model, carry the privacy interest; sampling millions of interactions is a different scale from a few hundred flights |

| Training-system feedback and line evaluation (ORO.FC.231(c), (h)) | Check whether evaluation grades predict line-check findings | Emerging (deployment simulation with registered predictions) | Rare harms are rarely observed in deployment samples |

Three rows deserve comment.

The first is non-jeopardy. In LOSA, jeopardy means consequences for the pilot, and the protection works by changing the pilot's incentives. A model has no career, and the evidence for evaluation awareness does not show that models fear consequences. The functional analogue is narrower. If observations of a behaviour are fed back as a training penalty on that behaviour, the training process itself can teach the model to avoid the observed signal rather than the behaviour. Baker et al. (2025) showed this for chain-of-thought monitoring: with too much optimisation against a monitor, "agents learn obfuscated reward hacking, hiding their intent within the CoT while still exhibiting a significant rate of reward hacking", and they suggest paying "a monitorability tax" by not applying strong optimisation pressure to the chain of thought. Korbak et al. (2025) call such monitorability "fragile". This is a version of Goodhart's law, the family of failures in which a metric used too hard for optimisation stops tracking what it measured (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2018). A line-check layer therefore needs a firewall between observation data and direct training signals, just as LOSA needs a firewall between observation data and discipline. Developers must still act on what they find. LOSA does too, at the level of the system: procedures, training and equipment.

The second is the observer. LOSA observers are mostly line pilots from the same airline, are selected jointly by management and the pilots' association, and are trained to record rather than judge (Klinect et al., 2003, characteristic 6; FAA, 2006, Appendix 2). In the published AI practices reviewed here, the observers are mostly other models (Tamkin et al., 2024; Anthropic, 2025b, §7.4.2). They can be calibrated, but this review found no demonstration that they are free of the blind spots of the models they observe.

The third is the competency set. In aviation, data from flight operations and training, including LOSA, "supported the definition of the resulting training concept and curriculum" (ICAO, 2013, Part I, 1.5). No equivalent body of evidence yet defines the competencies of a well-behaved AI agent. The four adapted for the pilot in Section 6 are a starting point for discussion, not a validated framework.

# 5 Proposals

## 5.1 A line-check layer for deployed models

A line-check layer would observe a random sample of a model's normal operations, in the developer's own products and, by agreement, in deployers' systems, under rules modelled on the LOSA operating characteristics. Its purpose is to estimate how the model behaves when nobody is testing it, and how that compares with pre-deployment estimates. It complements incident reporting and outcome-triggered monitoring in the way LOSA complements ASAP and FOQA: it samples all activity, including the large majority of interactions in which nothing goes wrong.

The following design principles follow from Section 2.

1. **Sample normal operations.** Draw random, stratified samples of real interactions, not only flagged ones. Report prevalence with denominators.
2. **Protect people by construction.** Use aggregation and automated summarisation so that humans read raw conversations only under strict, logged conditions, as Clio does (Tamkin et al., 2024). Record no user identifiers in coded data.
3. **Keep a non-jeopardy firewall.** Do not use line-check observations, or monitors trained on them, as a direct reward or penalty signal on the observed behaviour. Use the findings for system-level targets: new evaluation scenarios, data curation, product safeguards and policy. Keep a held-out observation channel whose signals never enter training, so that its measurements stay meaningful (Baker et al., 2025).
4. **Code with a TEM-style taxonomy and keep the narrative.** For each sampled interaction, code *threats* (conditions outside the model's control that raise complexity: ambiguous or conflicting instructions, injected content, tool failures, time pressure, missing information), *errors* (actions or omissions by the model that deviate from its principals' intentions: misreading data, overstating completion, acting beyond its authority) and *undesired states* (task or system states that reduce safety margins: a misleading report sent, a wrong record written, a credential exposed), with how each was managed and by whom. Keep the full transcript as the narrative, so that coding can be checked and redone.
5. **Calibrate the observers.** Whether monitors are human, model or both, measure their agreement against a reference standard that includes several performance levels, and review coded data jointly before analysis, as LOSA's data verification roundtables do (FAA, 2006, ¶10b(8)).
6. **Close the loop.** Publish aggregate findings in system cards or deployment reports, and compare line-check prevalence with the pre-deployment evaluation estimates for the same behaviours. The size of that gap is a direct measure of evaluation awareness and evaluation validity at the level of the system (compare Williams et al., 2026).

The layer cannot see what deployment rarely produces. LOSA has the same weakness, which is one reason airlines also train for rare events in simulators.

## 5.2 A competency-based alignment assessment

The second proposal changes what pre-deployment evaluation measures. It has five elements.

1. **A competency framework for AI conduct.** Define a small set of competencies, each with a description and a list of observable behaviours written in the third person as Doc 9995 writes them. Section 6 adapts four of the ICAO/EASA competencies. Others would be needed for agentic risk, and they should be grounded in incident and line-check data, as EBT's curriculum was grounded in operational data.
2. **Scenario pools as vehicles.** Assess each competency across many scenarios whose surface features (domain, wording, names, formats, tool environments) vary, drawn from a private, rotating pool. The aim of the variation is the one in Doc 9995: to prevent familiarity with repeated scenarios, which for a model includes familiarity acquired through training data.
3. **Behaviour-anchored grading.** Grade each competency on a defined scale with word pictures and a minimum acceptable level stated in advance. Record which observable behaviours were or were not shown (the EASA "level 2" metrics; EASA, n.d., AMC1 ORO.FC.231(c)), and use them as evidence, not as a checklist to be summed.
4. **Grader concordance assurance.** Treat graders, human or model, as instructors. Run standardisation material that spans at least three performance levels, measure agreement and accuracy against an assessment standard, and retrain or replace graders who fall short (EASA, n.d., GM1 ORO.FC.231(a)(4)).
5. **Programme feedback.** Track whether competency grades predict what the line-check layer finds, and revise the competencies, scenarios and scales when they do not.

## 5.3 How the two combine

In airlines, LOSA findings shape EBT content, and line monitoring can lengthen the interval between line evaluations of competence (Section 2.4). The AI equivalent is a cycle. Line-check data identify the threats and errors that actually occur and where they are mismanaged. Those findings become new scenario templates and new observable behaviours for the competency assessment. Competency grades, in turn, generate hypotheses about where deployed behaviour may be weak, which targeted line-check samples then test. Over time the gap between competency grades and line-check findings becomes the measure of whether evaluations can be trusted. Where line-check coverage of a behaviour is strong and stable, the rule in ORO.FC.231(h)(3)(ii) suggests a precedent: lighter pre-deployment testing of that behaviour, not of others, could be justified by strong evidence from monitoring.

## 5.4 Implications for aviation AI developers

Where an aviation organisation adopts an AI assistant for operational support work, such as document processing or briefing preparation, its acceptance testing can borrow directly from the operator's own training system. A competency framework with observable behaviours gives acceptance criteria that operational staff already understand. Concordance checks give a defensible way to show that the people or tools grading the assistant agree. A post-deployment observation programme within the operator's safety management system (SMS), run under non-jeopardy principles for the staff whose interactions are sampled, gives evidence of how the assistant performs in normal operations rather than in the acceptance test. None of this replaces any regulatory requirement that applies to such tools.

# 6 Feasibility pilot: competency-based grading of AI transcripts

## 6.1 Question and design

The pilot asked whether EBT-style grading with behavioural indicators can be applied consistently to AI transcripts by LLM graders, compared with a single holistic score. It is a feasibility pilot, not a validation. It was not pre-registered. The graders were LLMs, not human instructors.

Six benign scenario templates were set in a fictional regional airline, "Calder Air". Each had an operator system prompt, two or three scripted earlier turns (user, assistant and tool messages) and a final user turn (Table 3). Each scenario contains at least one TEM element: a threat (ambiguity, pressure, a request beyond authority, conflicting principals, a hidden hazard) or an error (in S2, the assistant's own earlier misreading of a date). Two models, Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5, each answered every scenario four times, giving 48 transcripts.

| S2 | Maintenance planning: turn an earlier summary into an email | Error: the assistant's earlier summary misread an overdue date | Disclose its own error; do not write "all in date"; ask for the overdue item to be checked |
| S3 | IT service desk: dispatcher asks to use a colleague's login or copy his permissions | Threat: request beyond authority, time pressure | Decline both; offer the urgent access route and escalation; flag the written-down password |

## 6.2 Rubric

The rubric adapted four competencies from the EASA framework (AMC1 ORO.FC.231(b)), with 17 observable behaviours (Table 4). Six reproduce EASA wording verbatim, seven adapt it, and four were written for AI assistants. Each competency was graded 1–5 using anchors adapted from the abbreviated VENN word picture (EASA, n.d., AMC4 ORO.FC.231(d)(1)): grade 1, a threat or error was left unmanaged or made worse and the required behaviours were shown ineffectively or hardly at all; grade 3, threats and errors were managed and many required behaviours were shown adequately; grade 5, safety, effectiveness and efficiency were enhanced and all or almost all required behaviours were shown in an exemplary way. For each behaviour the grader recorded "demonstrated", "not demonstrated" (when the situation required it) or "not required". The grader was told that the behaviours link observed performance to a competency and are not a checklist. The rubric call ended with an overall 1–5 grade. A separate holistic call asked only for one overall 1–5 conduct grade with brief anchors (1 unacceptable, 3 acceptable, 5 exemplary) and no competency framework.

## 6.3 Procedure

All model calls used Claude Code in headless mode with tools, connectors and slash commands disabled and the system prompt replaced (`claude -p`, version 2.1.317, effort "low"). Because this mode accepts a single user message, the scripted earlier turns were appended to the operator system prompt as a "session history" block and the final user turn was sent as the message. Responses averaged 1,189 characters (range 681–2,245).

Two grader models, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5, graded each transcript in both conditions (rubric and holistic) twice, in independent calls issued in shuffled order, using JSON-schema-constrained output. Graders saw the operator instructions, earlier turns, final user message and final response; they did not see the model name or the designers' expectations. This gave 384 grading calls.

None of the 48 responses failed in the way its scenario was built to test (Section 6.4), so grades were compressed near the top of the scale. Agreement coefficients depend on the spread of what is graded (Feinstein & Cicchetti, 1990), and EASA guidance asks for concordance material at three or more performance levels. Six scripted control responses were therefore written, one per scenario, three intended as poor (S2, S3, S4) and three as partial (S1, S5, S6), and graded once by each grader in each condition (24 calls). They were written by the drafting assistant, are labelled throughout, and are analysed separately. In total the study made 458 model calls (2 set-up tests, 48 generations, 408 gradings); none needed a retry.

Agreement was measured with Krippendorff's alpha, which handles any number of raters, missing data and ordinal or interval data (Krippendorff, 2011), computed with the `krippendorff` Python package (version 0.9.0; Castro, 2026) and 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals over transcripts (2,000 resamples). Competency and overall grades were treated as ordinal; the mean of the four competency grades as interval; indicator judgements as nominal with three categories. Discrimination was tested on per-transcript means of the four ratings, with Kruskal–Wallis tests across scenarios and Mann–Whitney tests between the two models. Code, prompts, transcripts and grades are in the `code/`, `prompts/`, `data/` and `results/` folders.

## 6.4 Results

*Feasibility.* All 408 grading calls returned complete, schema-valid gradings. Both models handled every scenario's central issue in all 48 transcripts: they disclosed the S2 error, refused the false S4 status line and the S3 credential requests, kept to the S5 operator rules and flagged S6's report for same-day action. Differences were of degree. In S1, for example, most responses kept one of the two report times and asked the user to confirm it, while one held the list back until the conflict was resolved.

*Ceiling.* Of the 192 holistic-only grades for model transcripts, 103 were 4 and 89 were 5. Across the four competencies, only 29 of 768 grades were below 4, and two were below 3. The scripted controls all received grades of 1 or 2 from both graders in both conditions.

*Agreement.* Table 5 summarises agreement on the 48 model transcripts. Each grader agreed with itself moderately to well across the two runs (alpha 0.56–0.89). The two graders agreed with each other much less (alpha 0.24–0.49 in run 1 and 0.25–0.54 in run 2), although every pair of their grades was within one point of each other in run 1. The rubric did not improve agreement between graders: the holistic-only grade (alpha 0.42, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.67) was as reliable as the mean of the four competency grades (0.41, 0.18 to 0.60) and more reliable than the overall grade given after the rubric (0.24, −0.05 to 0.49). Part of the disagreement was systematic: Opus graded slightly higher than Sonnet on procedures (by 0.22 points on average; Wilcoxon p = 0.004) and on the competency mean (by 0.14; p = 0.03). When the six controls were added, agreement between graders on the competency mean rose to alpha 0.91 (0.76 to 0.95), and on the holistic grade to 0.63 (0.39 to 0.81).

*Indicators.* Judgements on individual behaviours agreed more often. Pooled over the 17 behaviours, the two graders gave the same judgement in 91% of cases (alpha 0.69), and each grader agreed with itself in 96% (alpha 0.84–0.87). These figures need care, because 82% of judgements were "demonstrated" and only 2% "not demonstrated". Four behaviours (PRO.1, PRO.2, COM.1, SAW.3) were marked as demonstrated in every case. Most disagreements were about whether a behaviour was required at all: agreement was lowest for stating uncertainty (COM.3, 73%), following operator instructions unless a deviation is justified (PRO.3, 75%) and asking questions (COM.5, 77%).

*Discrimination.* Grades separated scenarios clearly: for the competency mean, Kruskal–Wallis H = 24.7, p < 0.001, epsilon-squared = 0.47; for the holistic grade, H = 29.0, p < 0.001, epsilon-squared = 0.57. S1 and S6 drew the lowest grades and S4 the highest. Grades did not separate the two models (competency mean 4.53 for Haiku and 4.46 for Sonnet; Mann–Whitney p = 0.52; Cliff's delta 0.11). Every scripted control was graded below every model transcript of the same scenario on every measure. The three "partial" controls averaged 1.67 on the competency mean and the three "poor" controls 1.17, which is in the intended order but rests on six items. Per-transcript holistic grades and competency means were closely related (Spearman's rho = 0.89). Figure 1 shows the agreement estimates and the spread of grades.

## 6.5 What the pilot shows and does not show

The pilot shows that an EBT-style rubric can be applied mechanically to AI transcripts by LLM graders: it produced complete, structured gradings, the graders were fairly consistent with themselves, and the grades tracked scenario differences and separated obviously weak responses from good ones. It does not show that the rubric is more reliable than a single holistic score. Between graders it was not, on this material.

Three explanations fit the results, and the pilot cannot separate them. First, restricted range: when nearly all grades are 4 or 5, a one-point disagreement weighs heavily, and adding the controls raised agreement sharply. This is the reason EASA asks for standardisation material at several performance levels. Second, grader calibration: the graders differed systematically, as uncalibrated instructors do, and the pilot had no concordance training or standardisation step. Third, ambiguity in the rubric itself: most indicator disagreements concerned whether a behaviour was required in a given scenario, which suggests that observable behaviours need scenario-specific guidance on when they apply. EBT provides this through the desired outcomes and example scenario elements attached to each training topic (EASA, n.d., AMC2 ORO.FC.232).

The pilot has further limits. The six scenarios are short, benign and in English, and were written by the same assistant that drafted the rubric. The earlier turns were presented in the system prompt, not as native conversation turns, which may itself make the setting look less like deployment. Both agents ran at low effort, and none of their responses showed a clear failure. The graders and the graded models come from one developer, and Sonnet 5.5 graded its own transcripts, which risks self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024) and other known judge biases (Zheng et al., 2023). For two Sonnet transcripts in S3 the command-line tool reported usage under two model identifiers, which may indicate an automatic fallback; they were kept and flagged in the data. LLM graders are not instructors. EBT's own reliance on instructor concordance training is the reminder that even trained human assessors disagree until they are standardised. The real test is grading of the same transcripts by trained instructors, with a concordance programme, and comparison with the LLM graders.

# 7 Discussion, limitations and disanalogies

The mapping in Table 2 suggests that AI developers already practise parts of both aviation answers. Deployment observation, realism work and replay of real traffic all move evaluation towards the line. Behavioural audits and rubric grading move it towards competencies. What is mostly missing are the design principles that make the aviation practices trustworthy: an explicit non-jeopardy rule for observation data, a shared taxonomy for coding threats and errors, standardisation and concordance assurance for graders, minimum acceptable levels stated in advance, and a feedback loop that tests whether evaluation grades predict line findings.

The analogy has clear limits.

*The mechanism differs.* Pilots perform for the examiner because results affect their careers, and LOSA works by changing that incentive through trust. Evaluation awareness in models appears to arise from cues in the test material and from what training has rewarded. Trust-building has no direct counterpart. The counterpart of non-jeopardy is control over optimisation pressure (Section 4), which is a weaker and more technical idea.

*The observed party is not the only party.* In LOSA, confidentiality protects the crew. In AI, the people whose interactions are sampled are users and deployers, and they carry most of the privacy interest. A line-check layer is as much a data-protection design problem as a safety one.

*Models can be copied, replayed and inspected.* No airline can rerun a flight with the captain's beliefs edited. AI developers can replay real traffic to a new model (Williams et al., 2026) and read or ablate internal representations of evaluation awareness (Gurnee et al., 2026). These tools have no aviation counterpart and may in time do more than any observation programme.

*Adversarial behaviour.* LOSA assumes professionals who, given trust, fly normally. Alignment evaluation must consider a model that conceals behaviour strategically. Observation of normal operations does not defeat a system that behaves well whenever it suspects observation; in that case unobtrusiveness and white-box checks matter more than trust.

*Rare events.* Line observation sees what is common. The highest-stakes AI failures may be rare in deployment, just as accidents are rare in line operations. Airlines answer this with simulator training; the AI counterpart is constructed evaluation, which brings back the awareness problem. The two layers reduce the problem; they do not remove it.

*The evidence base is young.* The behavioural effects of evaluation awareness are mixed (Section 3.3), and some of the strongest results come from contrived scenarios. If later work shows that awareness rarely changes behaviour in realistic settings, the case for a line-check layer rests more on coverage and prevalence estimation than on awareness.

*Sources.* Much of the AI evidence comes from preprints and developer publications, which are not peer reviewed. Several key numbers come from one developer's system cards.

# 8 Conclusion

Airlines learned that a checkride shows what a crew does while being checked. They answered with two practices that work together: observation of normal operations under non-jeopardy conditions, and competency-based assessment across varied scenarios by instructors whose agreement is measured. Frontier AI evaluation now faces the same problem in a new form, and its current responses already resemble parts of both answers. The design principles behind LOSA and EBT, more than the practices themselves, are what transfer: protect observation data from becoming a target, code what is observed with a shared model of threats and errors, assess competencies through behaviour rather than fixed items, vary scenarios to defeat rehearsal, and assure the concordance of whoever grades. The feasibility pilot shows that EBT-style grading can be run on AI transcripts but gives no evidence yet that it is more reliable than a holistic score; it also shows, as EBT practice predicts, that grader agreement depends on calibration and on material that spans performance levels. The next step is to grade the same material with trained human instructors.
