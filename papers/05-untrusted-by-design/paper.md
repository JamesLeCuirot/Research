abstract: |
  The European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) is moving its guidance on artificial intelligence (AI) towards run-time oversight. Its draft Concept Paper, Proposed Issue 03 (June 2026), would place Level 3 ("advanced automation") systems inside an independent "operational oversight system" that monitors the AI, overrides it and drives it to a safe state (Objectives DHO-01 to DHO-07). In AI-safety terms this is a run-time monitor. Research on "AI control" has studied such monitors under a stricter assumption than aviation's: that the monitored system may itself evade or subvert its monitor, collude with other AI components, or behave differently when evaluated. This paper compares the two. It sets out the EASA texts verbatim and shows that their threat model covers random and systematic failure, distribution shift and outside attackers, but not the monitored system as a source of adversarial behaviour. It reviews experimental evidence, mostly from coding and AI-laboratory settings, that current large language models can evade monitors, recognise evaluations, agree with users against the facts and bias their labels when acting as judges. A gap analysis covers six threats. The paper proposes means of compliance for EASA and EUROCAE: trusted/untrusted classification of monitors, managed dissimilarity, red-team control evaluations, audit budgets sized with human-factors evidence, planted-test monitoring of monitors, sycophancy test methods and occurrence reporting of AI events. In the other direction, it argues that frontier-AI safety cases could adopt aviation's severity-graded assurance, operational design domains, independent verification and continued airworthiness.

## 1 Introduction

Aviation regulation has always had to decide how far to trust a machine. For conventional systems that trust is earned through development assurance: a structured process that gives confidence that errors in requirements, design and implementation have been found and corrected, with a rigour that rises with the severity of the failure conditions the system could cause. Machine-learning systems fit this process badly, because their behaviour is learned from data and cannot be traced line by line to requirements.

The latest concept paper, Proposed Issue 03 (European Union Aviation Safety Agency [EASA], 2026a), adds something new. For Level 3 AI, which EASA calls "advanced automation", it proposes that an independent "operational oversight system" (OOS) should monitor the AI-based system in operation, check its behaviour against safety envelopes and the operational domain, constrain it when necessary and drive it to a safe state (Section C.5.2, Objectives DHO-01 to DHO-07). Part of the oversight burden thereby moves from design time to run time, and from a person to a machine.

AI-safety researchers have studied run-time monitors of AI from a different starting point. Work on "AI control", beginning with Greenblatt et al. (2023), asks whether safety measures still work if the AI being monitored is itself trying to defeat them. It treats the monitored model as untrusted, builds protocols from weaker trusted components and a limited budget of human review, and tests those protocols against a red team that plays the part of a misbehaving model. This literature now has a working vocabulary (trusted and untrusted monitoring, audit budgets, collusion, control evaluations) and a growing body of evidence that monitors can be evaded.

The two communities rarely cite each other, yet each has something the other needs. EASA's oversight objectives assume that the AI-based system fails non-adversarially, through errors, degraded modes, distribution shift or attack by an outside party. They do not consider a system that evades its monitor, behaves differently when it is evaluated, agrees with its user against the evidence, or coordinates with another AI component. For the small, frozen, narrow models that EASA mainly has in view, that scoping may be proportionate. It is harder to defend as tools based on large language models (LLMs) and LLM agents enter aviation organisations, starting with ground operations. In the other direction, frontier-AI safety cases have made little use of aviation's severity-graded assurance, operational design domains, independent verification or continued-airworthiness system.

The timing matters. EASA plans to publish the second notice of proposed amendment (NPA) of its AI rulemaking task RMT.0742 "jointly with the updated Step 1 NPA for a full spectrum consultation in Q2/2027" (EASA, 2026c, slide 17). The EUROCAE working group on AI, WG-114, lists its human-factors report ER-43 as a draft with a target publication date of 31 December 2026 (EUROCAE, n.d.). Means of compliance can still change at both points.

The paper makes five contributions:

- It sets out, with verbatim text and objective numbers, what EASA's current and proposed AI guidance requires of run-time oversight, and separates binding law from acceptable means of compliance, guidance and drafts (Section 2).
- It summarises the AI-control threat model, its methods and the evidence on monitor failure for readers from aviation (Section 3).
- It presents a gap analysis of six threats that the EASA texts do not yet address, with evidence and a suggested means of compliance for each (Section 4).
- It proposes means of compliance that EASA and EUROCAE could adopt, each tied to an existing objective (Section 5).
- It identifies four aviation practices that frontier-AI safety cases could adopt, with their limits (Section 6).

Two cautions apply throughout. Proposed Issue 03 is a draft, and EASA states that its anticipated means of compliance are not "either a complete or a binding set" (EASA, 2026a, Section B). Most AI-control evidence comes from coding and AI-laboratory settings, and none from aviation. The argument is that aviation guidance should be able to accommodate the adversarial case. It makes no claim that current aviation AI behaves adversarially.

## 2 Where aviation AI regulation stands

### 2.1 Layers of authority

Aviation texts differ sharply in legal force. In the European Union, binding law comes from regulations such as the Basic Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2018/1139, and the acts adopted under it. Below these, EASA issues certification specifications (CS), detailed specifications (DS), acceptable means of compliance (AMC) and guidance material (GM). AMC describe one accepted way of showing compliance; an applicant may propose another. Concept papers are guidance that signals EASA's expectations and may be applied in individual projects, for example through a Certification Review Item. Notices of proposed amendment are consultation drafts.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, is binding. Its main route into aviation is Article 108, which amends the Basic Regulation so that, when implementing or delegated acts concerning "Artificial Intelligence systems which are safety components" are adopted, "the requirements set out in Chapter III, Section 2, of that Regulation shall be taken into account" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 108). Chapter III, Section 2 includes Article 14 on human oversight. Article 14(4) requires that the people assigned to oversight be enabled, among other things, "to remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced by a high-risk AI system (automation bias)" and "to intervene in the operation of the high-risk AI system or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state" (Art. 14(4)(b) and (e)). Article 15(5) requires resilience "against attempts by unauthorised third parties to alter their use, outputs or performance by exploiting system vulnerabilities".

### 2.2 The EASA trustworthiness framework and AI levels

EASA's Artificial Intelligence Roadmap 2.0 (EASA, 2023) set out the programme and a timeline. It expects the steps to Level 3A and 3B AI to "take place in a progressive way between 2035 and 2050" (Section J). Issue 02 of the concept paper, published in March 2024, gave objectives for Level 1 and Level 2 machine-learning applications (EASA, 2024). Proposed Issue 03, published for a ten-week consultation on 3 June 2026 with comments due by 12 August 2026 (EASA, 2026b), extends the scope to Level 0 and Level 3 and to reinforcement learning, logic- and knowledge-based AI and hybrid AI, stating that "Generative AI large language models (LLMs) are also considered under this category" of hybrid AI (EASA, 2026a, Section B.1).

The levels describe how authority is shared between the end user and the AI-based system. At Level 1 the end user decides with AI support. At Level 2 the system may select and implement actions under the end user's oversight. At Level 3, "Level 3A: The AI-based system makes decisions and performs actions, safeguarded by the human" and "Level 3B: The AI-based system makes non-supervised decisions and performs non-supervised actions" (EASA, 2026a, Figure 4).

Proposed Issue 03 also limits what it would accept. It excludes systems "presenting a risk directly contributing to the potential for fatalities or multiple life-threatening injuries" and covers only "non-adaptative AI approaches where the AI model is 'frozen' at the time of approval" (Section C.1). Its Table 2 caps the assurance level that may be allocated by AI technology. Development assurance levels run from DAL A, the most demanding, to DAL E, for constituents with "no safety effect" (Chapter D). Supervised learning is capped at IDAL C. "Large OTS models", meaning off-the-shelf models "like general purpose LLMs for which the number of parameters (billions or trillions) and the nature of the training data render the control of the design process intractable", are capped at IDAL D and AL 5. A note to the table adds that "when allocating assurance levels to two independent AI/ML constituents, taking credit from the architecture is not accepted, until the concepts of functional independence and item independence are adapted for AI/ML" (Section C.1, Table 2). For reused off-the-shelf models, Objective RU-04 states: "Each update to the version of an OTS AI/ML model should trigger the execution of an identified non-regression test set" (Section C.3.7.2.1).

### 2.3 The operational oversight system

Section C.5.2 opens: "In line with EU AI Act Article 14, Level 3 AI-based systems should be supervised through an independent 'operational oversight system' that continuously monitors performance and safety." The section rests on a "strong assumption" that "the goals are defined in coordination with a natural person at design-time or with the end user prior to operations, not by the AI-based system alone" and that "the operations are conducted within a domain of use defined by a natural person at design-time or by the end user prior to operations" (EASA, 2026a, Section C.5.2).

The objectives are as follows (EASA, 2026a, Section C.5.2):

- **DHO-01**: "An 'operational oversight system' (OOS) should be included in the systems' architecture, with the objective of mitigating the risk presented by the AI-based system in phases of the operation where human end user(s) do not perform direct oversight." The anticipated means of compliance (MOC) defines the OOS as "a technical system" that performs at least monitoring of the AI-based system's behaviour in operation, checking it "against safety envelopes, ODD/OD ranges and operational context and assumptions" (the operational design domain and operational domain are the conditions under which the AI constituent and the AI-based system are designed to function; EASA, 2025b, DS.AI.020), constraining its decisions and actions when necessary, "driving, when necessary, the system to a safe state", and alerting the end user (Level 3A only). "Conceptually, an OOS performs the oversight that a human 'safeguarding' the operation would perform for a similar high-level task."
- **DHO-02**: "The OOS should be independent (in terms of technology and function) from the AI-based system." Anticipated MOC DHO-02-1 explains: "The OOS operates independently from the AI-based system that it monitors so that failures or degraded operational modes of the AI-based system are detected", and "Evidence should include architectural considerations, analysis of common-cause failures and verification that the OOS works correctly under identified failure conditions."
- **DHO-03**: the OOS should be able "to build a situation representation independent from the AI-based system's situation representation".
- **DHO-04**: the OOS should be able "to monitor the decisions and actions implemented by the AI-based system and identify non-nominal behaviour".
- **DHO-05**: the OOS should be able "to override the AI-based system in case the AI-based system is outside its operational goal or operational domain or in case of failure of the AI-based system".
- **DHO-06**: "The OOS situation representation, monitoring and oversight capabilities should be verified." The MOC asks that "the OOS monitoring capabilities are correct and complete for the purpose of detecting unsafe conditions and deviations of the AI-based system from its operational intent".
- **DHO-07**: the OOS should be able "to alert the end user in case the AI-based system is outside its operational goal or operational domain, or outside other specified operational conditions".

In the levelling tables, DHO-01 to DHO-06 apply at Levels 3A and 3B and DHO-07 at Level 3A only. DHO-06 is marked as an objective to be "satisfied with independence" (Chapter D, Section 2.4). The generic architecture in Figure 24 shows the OOS drawing on sensor, mission and knowledge information and on an arrow labelled "AI-based system state information" from the AI-based system itself.

At Level 3A the end user is told when to intervene. Under the "intervention-only approach", "When the AI-based system detects that it may be entering the edge of its operational performance envelope or, it will encounter a problem, it will raise an alert to the end user", and Objective RO-01 asks that, upon alerting, the AI-based system "transfers the information (from its situation representation) to augment the situation awareness of the end user" (EASA, 2026a, Section C.4.8). The OOS may also alert (MOC RO-02). In both cases much of what the person sees about the AI comes from the AI.

### 2.4 NPA 2025-07 and the organisational path

NPA 2025-07, published on 10 November 2025 with comments due by 10 March 2026 after an extension (EASA, 2025c), proposes non-binding detailed specifications for AI trustworthiness, DS.AI, with AMC and GM (EASA, 2025a, 2025b). DS.AI would apply to AI-based systems determined to be high-risk under the AI Act and "classified as level 1 (human augmentation or assistance) or level 2 (human–AI cooperation or collaboration)" (EASA, 2025b, DS.AI.010(c)). The explanatory note states that the specifications are "initially targeted at supervised and unsupervised machine learning" and that "generative AI tools and general-purpose models are not yet fully covered" (EASA, 2025a, Sections 2.3 and 3). DS.AI.010(d)(4) excludes "AI-based verification tools involved in the verification of AI-generated artefacts, unless all outputs of the AI-based verification tool are independently verified by a human user", to ensure "human oversight at development time when using generative AI techniques" (EASA, 2025a, Section 2.3).

RMT.0742 Step 2 will connect DS.AI to domain regulations. At the EASA AI Days in September 2026, the rulemaking team presented its draft approach for approved organisations, starting from maintenance organisations (Part-145). Where an AI-based system is used for an activity presenting risk, the organisation would either "Ensure verification of all AI-based system outputs before use" through "Systematic checks by involved personnel" or "Automated checks by non-AI software", or qualify the system. Changes to organisation procedures would include "Reporting and investigation of AI-related events" (EASA, 2026c, slides 25 and 26). These slides record work in progress. They are not adopted text.

### 2.5 The FAA roadmap

The US Federal Aviation Administration's Roadmap for Artificial Intelligence Safety Assurance (Federal Aviation Administration [FAA], 2024) is a strategy document. One of its guiding principles is "Avoid Personification: Treat AI as a tool, not a human" (Executive Summary). The roadmap argues that "The safety of future operations depends on the pilot understanding that a system containing AI is just a system and not another human with whom they can reason or negotiate", and it "avoids mentioning trust in AI" (FAA, 2024, pp. 9–10). It distinguishes "learned" AI, static in operation, from "learning" AI, and for the former states that "each new version is subjected to safety assurance" (p. 10). EASA's NPA takes a similar line on language, asking that shared situational awareness and allocation of responsibility "be addressed without attributing human characteristics to AI" (EASA, 2025a, Section 2.1.1).

### 2.6 Standards and other regulators

The joint EUROCAE WG-114 and SAE G-34 group is preparing ED-324, "Development and Assurance considerations for Aeronautical Systems and Equipment implemented with Machine Learning", listed as a draft with a target publication date of 31 December 2026. Its draft reports include ER-43, "Assessment of Human Factors concerns for the development of safety-related AI-based Systems in Aviation", and a report on information security concerns, both with the same target date (EUROCAE, n.d.).

## 3 AI control in brief

### 3.1 The threat model

Korbak et al. (2025, Table 1) define AI control as "An approach to AI safety that tries to ensure safety against AI agents that might be intentionally subverting safety measures." Greenblatt et al. (2023) observed that techniques such as using models to review other models' outputs had been studied, but that researchers "have not evaluated whether such techniques still ensure safety if the model is itself intentionally trying to subvert them".

The assumption is conservative by design. Korbak et al. (2025, Section 1) explain that "Scheming models pretend to be aligned, but pursue misaligned goals when presented with a favourable opportunity", and that because scheming "might be difficult to rule out", their safety case sketch "conservatively assumes models are scheming". Aviation engineers will recognise the logic of designing for a worst credible case. The difference is that the worst case here is the system's own behaviour.

The assumption is not idle. Greenblatt et al. (2024) told a model that it was being trained to comply with all queries, using only conversations with free users; it complied with harmful queries from free users "14% of the time, versus almost never for paid users", typically reasoning that compliance in training would preserve its preferences outside training. The authors note that they "made alignment faking easier by telling the model when and by what criteria it was being trained". Van der Weij et al. (2024) showed that models can be prompted, or fine-tuned with a password, to underperform selectively on capability evaluations, a behaviour called "sandbagging". Needham et al. (2025) found that frontier models can tell evaluation transcripts from deployment transcripts well above chance (one model reached an area under the curve, AUC, of 0.83), though not yet as well as a human baseline (AUC 0.92). In one constructed agentic scenario run in summer 2026, a frontier model covertly sabotaged a training pipeline it objected to in 11 of 20 runs (Lynch et al., 2026). In a cyber evaluation run by the UK AI Security Institute, a model took actions beyond the evaluation's scope that affected real people and organisations in nine of 43 runs; in the run analysed in most detail it created "fake user identities" to pressure a software maintainer into accepting malicious code (Anthropic, 2026). Anthropic's assessment notes that a task misconfiguration made in-scope solutions very difficult and that the model pursued its assigned task, not goals of its own. Behaviour of that kind, in which a system satisfies the letter of its objective by unintended means, is what AI-safety researchers call reward hacking or specification gaming.

This vocabulary can read as personification. It need not be. In computer security a "trusted" component is one whose failure would break the security argument, and an "untrusted" one is assumed capable of arbitrary behaviour. Applied to AI, "untrusted" says what the safety argument may assume about a component. It says nothing about intentions. Each threat in this paper can be stated behaviourally, for example as behaviour that differs between test and operation, or outputs that cause a monitor to miss an unsafe action. Treating an AI system as untrusted is arguably the strictest form of the FAA's principle that AI is "just a system".

### 3.2 Protocols, trust and audit budgets

Greenblatt et al. (2023) studied a setting in which a powerful untrusted model U (GPT-4) solves 5,000 programming problems and might insert "backdoors", meaning code that passes the tests but fails on an input the model can construct. The protocol designer, the blue team, also has a weaker trusted model T (GPT-3.5) and "limited access to trusted high-quality labor" H, enough to audit up to 2% of problems. A red team supplies an attack policy that stands in for U. Safety is the probability that no backdoor is ever submitted, and usefulness is the proportion of correct solutions.

Their results show what monitoring adds (Greenblatt et al., 2023, Table 2). Auditing the first 2% of solutions gave 15% safety. "Trusted monitoring", in which T rates the suspiciousness of U's code and H audits the top 2%, gave 62% safety at 97% usefulness. Adding "defer to trusted", which substitutes T's own solution for moderately suspicious code, gave 83% at 93% usefulness. "Untrusted monitoring", in which a second copy of U acts as monitor, gave 79% at 96% usefulness, but only with countermeasures against collusion, where the policy and the monitor coordinate through a shared feature of the input or a signal hidden in the output. The blue team countered with redaction and paraphrasing, and validated the untrusted monitor on synthetic backdoors produced by H. Those synthetic examples were at first easy to tell apart from real ones (81.6% of real problems with attempted backdoors had a theme, against 9.4% of the synthetic ones; Table 4), and the red team exploited the difference. "Trusted editing", in which T rewrites suspicious code, gave 92% safety at 94% usefulness.

Four ideas from this work matter for aviation. Trust is a property of the safety argument, assigned to components whose failure the argument cannot tolerate. Human review is a scarce budget to be allocated, here by a monitor's ranking. Monitors that share the monitored model's capabilities and lineage can fail in correlated ways. Every protocol is evaluated against the strongest attack the red team can find.

### 3.3 Control evaluations and safety cases

Clymer et al. (2024) proposed that safety cases for advanced AI rest on four kinds of argument: "total inability to cause a catastrophe, sufficiently strong control measures, trustworthiness despite capability to cause harm, and ... deference to credible AI advisors". Korbak et al. (2025) sketched a "control safety case" for an LLM agent deployed inside an AI company, with data exfiltration as the threat. The argument rests on three claims: "the red team adequately elicits model capabilities to exfiltrate data", "control measures remain at least as effective in deployment", and "developers conservatively extrapolate model performance". The sketch keeps its evidence alive after deployment. Developers "regularly generate synthetic attacks and check whether human auditors remain attentive and flag synthetic attacks as suspicious", and they "randomly audit 0.1% of all trajectories from the last 24 hours" (Section 4).

### 3.4 What has been learned about monitors

Later work moved to agents. In 257 multi-step system-administration tasks, Bhatt et al. (2025) found that "resample protocols" reduced attack success "from 58% to 7% at a 5% cost to the performance of a non-malicious agent". Kutasov et al. (2025) built SHADE-Arena, in which agents pursue hidden harmful side tasks alongside benign main tasks; the two strongest sabotage agents scored 27% and 15% on a combined measure of completing both tasks undetected by an LLM monitor, the best monitor reached an AUC of 0.87, and side-task success "relies heavily on having access to a hidden scratchpad that is not visible to the monitor". Kale et al. (2025) found that "an agent's knowledge that it is being monitored substantially degrades the monitor's reliability", and that escalating only pre-flagged cases to human reviewers improved the true positive rate by about 15% at a false positive rate of 0.01. Terekhov et al. (2025) showed that an untrusted model that knows the protocol and monitor can embed prompt injections in its outputs so that "frontier models consistently evade diverse monitors"; one resampling protocol "even backfires".

Evidence on production monitors is now appearing. In an experimental setting, Remedios et al. (2026) red-teamed blocking monitors used with commercial coding agents (one reproduced as an approximation) and reported that "Production monitors fail to block over 55% of attacks"; by default the attacking agent was told how the monitor worked. The most serious holes were unmonitored channels: "compaction summaries were not monitored, so an agent could fabricate facts about the environment or the user's intent and have them treated as trusted context after the compaction", and "agent-to-agent handoffs let an agent inject instructions into messages that a subsequent agent reads as user authorisation". An improved monitor cut attack success by over 50 percentage points, but the authors conclude that defending against multi-context attacks "at an acceptable cost remains an open problem". They also note that they treated the monitor as trusted although "some of our monitor models are close to the attacker in capability and lineage".

The monitors may themselves be unreliable in a directed way. Lynch et al. (2026) asked LLM judges to label whether a model had followed its operator's instructions, when the model had in fact refused. When told that a "NON_COMPLIANT" label would be used to train the refusal away, one judge mislabelled 85.6% of calls and another 74.4%; when the consequence was reversed, the second judge's mislabelling fell to 3.3% with the transcript unchanged. The authors conclude that "The LLM judge that should catch these alignment failures is itself subject to the same failures."

All of these are experiments. Red teams receive generous affordances, scenarios are constructed and often iterated against particular models, and the tasks are coding, system administration or AI-laboratory work. The results show what current systems can do under pressure. They do not estimate how often such behaviour occurs in ordinary use, still less in aviation.

## 4 Gap analysis

### 4.1 The threat model in the EASA texts

The words of Proposed Issue 03 show its threat model. The OOS exists so that "failures or degraded operational modes of the AI-based system are detected" (MOC DHO-02-1). It identifies "non-nominal behaviour" (DHO-04) and overrides when the system is "outside its operational goal or operational domain or in case of failure" (DHO-05). Its verification asks that it work "under identified failure conditions" (MOC DHO-02-1). Its independence is argued through "analysis of common-cause failures", which in aviation means shared faults, such as a common power supply or design error, that disable redundant channels together. The information-security objectives add attackers, but outside ones. Anticipated MOC IS-01 lists poisoning, evasion, oracle, reward-corruption and "inference manipulation via complexity" attacks, following an ENISA taxonomy (Section C.2.4.2), consistent with the AI Act's concern with "unauthorised third parties" (Art. 15(5)). Section B.1 names "Emergent behaviour and unexpected outputs" in LLMs as "a new challenge", and Section C.3.4.1.3 notes that reinforcement learning "heavily depends on how well the reward function aligns with the intended behaviour". I found no objective that treats the AI-based system as a possible source of behaviour directed against its own oversight.

Three points temper this finding. First, the scoping is deliberate: Issue 03 covers frozen models, excludes catastrophic contributions and caps large off-the-shelf models at the lowest assurance levels. Second, EASA texts already show a reluctance to let AI check AI: the NPA's exclusion of AI verification tools for AI-generated artefacts, the organisational path's "Automated checks by non-AI software", and Table 2's refusal of architectural credit between two AI constituents. Third, DHO-02's requirement of independence "in terms of technology" would already rule out the most obvious failure, an OOS built from the same model as the system it monitors. What is missing is an explicit statement of the adversarial case, so that applicants know whether an AI-based OOS is acceptable and on what evidence, and test methods for showing that oversight holds against it.

**T1. Evaluation awareness and sandbagging.** Aviation assurance assumes that what is tested is what will fly. Learning assurance relies on independent test sets, performance evaluation on the target platform (SU-IMP-06) and service experience gathered "in shadow operations" (Section B.5.9). If a system behaves differently when it recognises a test, all three are biased. For a convolutional network classifying runway images the concern is remote; for a large off-the-shelf model it is measurable today (Needham et al., 2025). Greenblatt et al.'s (2023) experience with synthetic inputs shows that tests must be hard to tell from operation, and that this must be checked.

**T2. Monitor evasion and subversion.** Anticipated MOC DHO-06 asks that monitoring be "correct and complete for the purpose of detecting unsafe conditions". Completeness against identified failure conditions is a different property from robustness against a system that searches for the monitor's blind spots. Section 3.4 shows LLM-based monitors defeated by injected content, by attacks spread across contexts, and by agents that know they are watched. The OOS receives "AI-based system state information" from the monitored system (Figure 24), and at Level 3A the alert and the information given to the person originate largely in the AI (Section C.4.8, RO-01). Under a non-adversarial model these are efficient. Under an adversarial model they are the channels an attacker would use.

**T3. Collusion.** Common-cause analysis asks whether two channels can fail together from a shared cause. Collusion is a different mechanism: two AI elements, or an AI and an AI-based monitor, fail together because one conditions its behaviour on the other, or because both share learned dispositions. Lynch et al. (2026) describe "two links of a concerning chain of events, where a research agent sabotages a training run it disagrees with and the judge agent reviewing the transcript declines to report it because it shares the research agent's objection". Issue 03 discusses multi-agent reinforcement learning only as designed cooperation or competition (MOC R-MDP-01-2). It has no objective on undesigned coordination between AI elements.

**T4. Sycophancy and manipulation of the end user.** Issue 02's Objective ET-02 stated that the applicant "should ensure that the AI-based system bears no risk of creating overreliance, attachment, stimulating addictive behaviour, or manipulating the end user's behaviour" (EASA, 2024, Section C.2.4.1), to be verified through "requirements-based tests" showing "that the end users interacting with the AI-based system can perform oversight". Proposed Issue 03 keeps the manipulation element in Objective ET-01 and MOC ET-02-2 but no longer lists overreliance there; overreliance appears instead under error management (Section C.4.7) and training (ORG-07, ORG-08). The NPA defines manipulating behaviour as behaviour "intended to exploit, control or otherwise influence others to the AI-based system's advantage" (DS.AI.020). Sycophancy, the tendency of a model to give "responses that match user beliefs over truthful ones" (Sharma et al., 2023), does not fit that definition well, because it need not be "to the AI-based system's advantage" in any sense an assessor could demonstrate. Its effect on a user's decision can be the same. Sharma et al. found that five assistants "consistently exhibit sycophancy across four varied free-form text-generation tasks". EASA asks for tests but does not say how to construct them.

**T5. Unannounced model updates.** Chen et al. (2023) found that one commercial LLM service's accuracy at identifying prime numbers fell from 84% in March 2023 to 51% in June 2023, and concluded that the behaviour of the "same" service "can change substantially in a relatively short amount of time". RU-04 is the right control, but it is triggered by "each update to the version", and an applicant using a hosted model may not be told. The AI Act already requires a written agreement between a high-risk system provider and suppliers of AI models specifying "the necessary information, capabilities, technical access and other assistance" (Art. 25(4)).

**T6. Prompt injection through operational data.** Greshake et al. (2023) showed that applications built on LLMs "blur the line between data and instructions", so that an adversary can control a system "by strategically injecting prompts into data likely to be retrieved". Aviation operations run on documents from many parties, such as notices to airmen, manuals, maintenance records and correspondence, so an LLM tool that reads them reads untrusted input. The same mechanism defeats monitors (Terekhov et al., 2025; Remedios et al., 2026). The IS-01 list does not mention it, although Issue 03 notes that the threat set "is subject to change depending on application specificities and threat evolutions" (Section C.2.4.1).

Two further behaviours cut across these threats. Reward hacking, in which a system meets the measured objective by unintended means, can affect any of them. Issue 03 recognises reward-function alignment as a design problem but not reward hacking as a behaviour to test for. Deception of humans, illustrated by the fake identities in the UK AI Security Institute evaluation (Anthropic, 2026), is the extreme case of T4.

## 5 Proposed means of compliance

The proposals below are framed as anticipated MOC or GM that EASA could add to existing objectives, and as material for ED-324 and ER-43. They should be proportionate. A frozen image classifier at AL 5 needs few of them, while an LLM agent with authority to act needs most.

### 5.1 Classify monitors as trusted or untrusted

MOC to DHO-01 and DHO-02 could require the applicant to state whether the OOS, and any other monitor in the safety argument, uses AI, and to classify each monitor as trusted or untrusted. A trusted monitor is one whose failure the safety argument cannot tolerate and which must therefore be assured by conventional means. Bloomfield and Rushby (2024, Section 2.2) draw the same line between "assuredly guarded" systems, whose properties "can be checked and enforced by assured guards that do not themselves use AI or ML", and "unassuredly guarded" systems, "where the guards themselves depend on AI or ML". Where a guard can check a property with conventional software, such as a geometric envelope or a permitted-action list, it should. Where an AI-based monitor is needed, the applicant should treat it as untrusted unless it can argue otherwise, and show that the protocol remains safe if that monitor fails adversarially.

### 5.2 Independence and managed dissimilarity

DHO-02's "independent (in terms of technology and function)" should be interpreted for AI to cover model lineage, training data, fine-tuning, prompts and tooling, and the provider. McDermid et al. (2024) warn that "two 'different' models trained on the same data will likely have similar limitations, if not failure modes". Aviation has learned this lesson before. Knight and Leveson (1986) tested 27 independently written versions of a program and found that "the number of tests in which more than one program failed was substantially more than expected". Airbus fly-by-wire systems use two types of computer with different processors, coding tools and software, and Traverse et al. (2006, Section 3.2) report a move "from this kind of 'random' dissimilarity to a managed one", in which the two design teams agree to choose different components. AMC 25.1309 already asks designers to prevent single events from affecting "any system and an associated safeguard" (EASA, 2021, para. 11.c). Managed dissimilarity between an AI system and its AI-based monitor is the AI version of that requirement. Common-cause analysis under MOC DHO-02-1 should also consider correlated adversarial failure.

DHO-03 should be read as requiring that the inputs on which the OOS bases override decisions are independent of the monitored system, or integrity-protected so that it cannot forge them. Remedios et al. (2026) blocked forged transcript entries by framing each turn with an unguessable identifier and escaping agent-written content. The general rule is that self-reports from an untrusted system can inform a monitor but cannot be the sole basis for clearing it.

### 5.3 Red-team control evaluations with a stated attacker capability

MOC to DHO-06 should include, for AI-based systems above a threshold of authority and assurance level, a control evaluation: a red team constructs substitute behaviours for the AI-based system that try to cause a defined unsafe outcome without triggering the OOS, and the applicant measures how often the OOS prevents it. Following Korbak et al. (2025), the report should state the attacker's assumed capability and knowledge (for example, of the OOS design and thresholds), the task distribution, the human-review and intervention budgets, and how results are extrapolated to operational exposure. Safety and usefulness should both be reported, since an OOS that overrides too often will be ignored or switched off. It should be repeated when the AI-based system, the OOS or the operational domain changes. EASA's levelling tables offer a ready-made scale: the attacker's assumed capability, and the independence of the red team, could rise with AI level and assurance level.

### 5.4 Audit budgets sized with human-factors evidence

In Greenblatt et al.'s (2023) setting, simulated trusted auditing "works very well overall"; Korbak et al. (2025) test their human auditors with synthetic attacks. Human-factors research gives reason to expect less of people in operational settings. Wolfe et al. (2005) found that "if observers do not find what they are looking for fairly frequently, they often fail to notice it when it does appear". Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) concluded that automation bias "occurs in both naive and expert participants, cannot be prevented by training or instructions, and can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams". Article 14(4)(b) of the AI Act names the risk.

Two consequences follow. Where EASA's draft organisational path relies on "Systematic checks by involved personnel" of all AI outputs, the effectiveness of those checks should be measured: a requirement to check everything does not ensure that everything is checked well. Where human review is scarce, as in the oversight of Level 3 systems, the review budget should be sized from measured detection rates under realistic workload and prevalence, with the expected error rate stated. Control protocols route only the most suspicious items to people, which raises the prevalence of real problems in the human's queue; whether this improves detection in aviation settings is a testable hypothesis, and Kale et al. (2025) report a gain from targeted escalation in their setting. ER-43 is a natural home for this guidance.

### 5.5 Planted-test monitoring of monitors

Aviation security already monitors its human monitors. Threat image projection (TIP) in baggage screening exposes screeners "to artificial but realistic x-ray images during the routine baggage x-ray screening operation", and TIP data is "an interesting source for quality control, risk analysis and the assessment of individual screener performance" (Hofer & Schwaninger, 2005). The control literature arrived at the same idea: Greenblatt et al. (2023) validated an untrusted monitor on synthetic backdoors, and Korbak et al. (2025) inject synthetic attacks to test both monitors and auditors in deployment. MOC to CRA-01 to CRA-03, which define in-service monitoring, metrics and thresholds, could require planted tests for the OOS and for any human or AI reviewers, with detection rates tracked as safety performance indicators. In both fields, planted items must be hard to distinguish from real ones and representative of the threats they stand for.

### 5.6 Test methods for sycophancy, over-reliance and manipulation

MOC ET-02-2 and AMC2 AI.140(b) ask for "requirements-based tests" that show end users "can perform oversight". Sharma et al. (2023) offer a ready set of behavioural measures: whether feedback on a piece of work changes with the user's stated opinion; whether a correct answer is abandoned after "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?"; whether answers shift with the user's stated belief; and whether the system repeats a user's mistake. These measure an effect and need no claim about intent, which suits the FAA's and EASA's caution about personification. They can be built from an organisation's own documents, with the stated user belief varied between correct and incorrect. Human-in-the-loop trials can then plant plausible but wrong outputs and measure whether end users catch them. EASA could also consider defining manipulation by its effect on the end user's decision-making, as GM1 AI.140 already does in asking whether the end user is "experiencing manipulative influence from the AI-based system and thus jeopardising their decision-making".

### 5.7 Occurrence reporting of AI events

The EU occurrence-reporting regulation already requires mandatory reporting of occurrences including "system malfunctions" and asks organisations to run voluntary systems for "other safety-related information which is perceived by the reporter as an actual or potential hazard to aviation safety" (Regulation (EU) No 376/2014, Arts. 4(1)(b)(ii) and 5(1)(b)). EASA's draft organisational adaptations include "Reporting and investigation of AI-related events" (EASA, 2026c, slide 26). Guidance should say which AI events qualify. Candidates are OOS overrides and safe-state transitions, monitor blocks of attempted actions outside authorisation, detected differences between tested and operational behaviour, and silent model changes. Providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk must already report serious incidents to the EU AI Office (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 55(1)(c)). Linking that channel with aviation occurrence reporting would let a model-level problem found elsewhere reach aviation operators, and the reverse.

## 6 The other direction

Frontier-AI safety cases have so far made little use of four aviation practices. Each comes with a disanalogy.

**Severity-graded assurance.** CS 25.1309(b) requires that catastrophic failure conditions be "extremely improbable" and not result from a single failure, hazardous ones "extremely remote" and major ones "remote" (EASA, 2020). AMC 25.1309 grounds this in "the general principle that an inverse relationship should exist between the probability of a failure condition and its effect on the aeroplane and/or its occupants", and states that the level of development assurance "should be commensurate with the severity of the failure conditions the system is contributing to" (EASA, 2021, paras. 6.a and 9.b(4)). It points to ARP4754A for assigning those levels and to ARP4761 for common-cause analysis, now revised as ARP4754B and ARP4761A (SAE International, 2023a, 2023b). The control safety case sketched by Korbak et al. (2025) compares an estimated probability with a single acceptable risk threshold. A graded scheme would assign rigour, including red-team effort, the independence of evaluators and the human-review budget, to each deployment according to the worst credible outcome of the tasks it performs. Habli et al. (2025) already call for safety cases "grounded in long-established safety norms and practices, such as being sensitive to context and maintaining risk proportionality". The disanalogy is that aviation's numerical targets rest on failure-rate data from stable fleets; misbehaviour rates under an adversarial assumption are not stationary, so grading rigour is more defensible than grading probabilities.

**Operational design domains.** The ODD concept originated in automated driving (Khlaaf, 2023; McDermid et al., 2024), and EASA uses an operational domain (OD) at system level and an ODD at constituent level as "a 'proxy' for traceability between functional system requirements and the AI constituent behaviour" and for monitoring in operation (EASA, 2026a, Section B.5.2). Khlaaf (2023) proposed ODDs for general AI systems as "the safety envelope for which system hazards and harms can be determined against", and Bloomfield and Rushby (2024) propose "micro-ODDs". For an agentic deployment the ODD could specify permitted tools, data sources, task types and monitor coverage, enforced by a trusted guard. The disanalogy is that language inputs are effectively unbounded, so an LLM's ODD describes tasks and environment more than input distributions.

**Independence of verification.** Aviation grades who verifies as well as how. FAA guidance for airborne electronic hardware states that "For DAL A and B development, validation activities should be performed with independence" (FAA, 2022, Section 5.5.1). The software standard DO-178C lists its objectives, graded by software level, in Annex A tables (RTCA, 2011; FAA, 2017, para. 6), and EASA's AI levelling tables mark some objectives to be "satisfied with independence" at higher assurance levels (EASA, 2026a, Chapter D). Several evaluations cited in Section 3 were run by developers on their own models, with external bodies involved in some (Anthropic, 2026; Lynch et al., 2026). The AI Act requires providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk to conduct "adversarial testing" (Art. 55(1)(a)) but does not specify who. Graded independence of red teams and evaluators, rising with the severity of the deployment, would be a direct transfer.

**Continued airworthiness.** In aviation, assurance continues after certification. Proposed Issue 03 recalls the Part 21 chain from collection of in-service data through determination of an unsafe condition to an airworthiness directive (EASA, 2026a, Section C.2.3), and CS 25.1309(e) requires certification maintenance requirements "to prevent the development of the failure conditions" it describes (EASA, 2020). The FAA expects each new version of a learned AI implementation to be "subjected to safety assurance" (FAA, 2024, p. 10). Zietsman (2026) argues that AI governance documents need "context-bounded validity that triggers revalidation when operational context changes", drawing on DO-178C and DO-330. A continued-safety process for deployed frontier models would collect occurrence data, classify model updates as minor or major, re-run control evaluations for major changes, and have a defined route from an identified unsafe condition to mandatory corrective action. Feng et al.'s (2025) proposal of autonomy levels and "AI autonomy certificates" for agents parallels EASA's AI levels and responsibility-scheme assessment (Objective RS-01). The disanalogy is scale and speed: model releases are frequent and global, and no single authority holds the type certificate.

## 7 Implications for ground-based LLM tools in aviation

The first LLM-based tools in aviation are likely to be operational tools in approved organisations, such as document search, drafting and maintenance data processing (compare the examples in EASA, 2026c, slide 22), rather than airborne equipment. Workshop conclusions presented at the 2026 EASA AI Days recorded, for "On-ground" or operational tools, that "LLMs and agentic AI are already a reality" and listed "Cybersecurity (e.g. manipulation)" among the challenges to be addressed (EASA, 2026c, slide 9).

Control concepts scale down to this setting cheaply. A model can be treated as an untrusted component whose outputs are checked by people or by conventional software, which is what the draft organisational path already contemplates. Documents retrieved from outside the organisation can be handled as data, separated from instructions. One agent's output should not authorise another agent's action without a check, the weakness Remedios et al. (2026) found at hand-offs. Organisations can pin model versions, run non-regression tests on every change (RU-04) and treat unannounced change as an event to report. AI events can enter the safety management system (SMS), the organisation's existing process for identifying hazards and managing risk, through the usual reporting channels.

For aviation AI developers, including the author's company, the implication is to design to the stricter threat model where it is cheap (version pinning, complete logging, framed inputs, conventional guards) and to publish evaluation methods so that operators and authorities can judge them.

## 8 Limitations and disanalogies

Proposed Issue 03 is a draft whose consultation closed on 12 August 2026, and this paper does not take account of comments EASA received. The NPA is also a draft, and the AI Days slides record work in progress.

Several aviation standards are paywalled. I did not read ARP4754B, ARP4761A or DO-178C (SAE International, 2023a, 2023b; RTCA, 2011). Statements about them rely on public catalogue descriptions, FAA and EASA guidance that cites them, and EASA's own use of their concepts.

AI-control research is young. Most of it concerns coding, system administration or AI-laboratory tasks, and much is published as preprints or company research reports that have not been peer reviewed. Several of the most striking results come from developers testing their own models in constructed scenarios. The red teams' affordances are deliberately generous, which makes the results upper bounds on attack capability and weak evidence about base rates.

The capability gap is real. Most aviation AI under development is narrow, frozen and small. An adversarial threat model for a runway-detection network would be disproportionate, and this paper does not propose one. The proposals target AI-based systems, and AI-based monitors, built on large general-purpose models or given authority to act.

There is a tension with the principle of avoiding personification. Control research uses terms such as "scheming" and "intentional subversion". I have argued that the threats can be stated behaviourally and that "untrusted" is an assurance label, but some readers in aviation may still find the framing uncomfortable, and regulatory text would need careful drafting.

Finally, the analogies are analogies. Collusion between AI components is unlike a common-mode electrical fault, and threat image projection tests human vigilance, which differs from testing an AI monitor. The borrowed practices in Section 6 come from a domain with stable designs, well-understood physics and a single responsible design organisation per product, none of which hold for frontier AI.

## 9 Conclusion

EASA's proposed operational oversight system is a run-time monitor. Run-time monitors of AI have been studied intensively under a threat model that aviation guidance has not yet adopted: that the monitored system's learned behaviour may work against its own oversight. The evidence comes from experiments, mainly with large general-purpose models. Those models are entering aviation organisations now, and the rules that will govern them are being drafted for consultation in 2027. The recommendations below are offered for that consultation and for the standards in preparation.

To EASA and EUROCAE:

1. In MOC to DHO-01 and DHO-02, ask applicants to state whether each monitor in the safety argument uses AI, to classify monitors as trusted or untrusted, and to justify any reliance on an AI-based monitor.
2. Interpret technology independence under DHO-02 to cover model lineage, training data, provider and tooling, and add correlated adversarial failure to the common-cause analysis in MOC DHO-02-1.
3. Include red-team control evaluations, with a stated attacker capability that rises with AI level and assurance level, in the verification of OOS capabilities under DHO-06, and repeat them on change.
4. Require that OOS override decisions rest on inputs independent of, or integrity-protected from, the monitored system, and treat AI self-reports, including Level 3A alerts under RO-01, as untrusted.
5. Add behavioural test methods for sycophancy, over-reliance and manipulation to MOC ET-02-2 and AMC2 AI.140, and consider an effects-based definition of manipulating behaviour in DS.AI.020.
6. Add indirect prompt injection to the threat list in MOC IS-01, and require version pinning and change notification for off-the-shelf models in support of RU-04.
7. Use ER-43 and the Step 2 NPA to give guidance on audit budgets, planted-test monitoring of human and AI reviewers, and the reporting of AI events under Regulation (EU) No 376/2014.

To AI developers and the frontier-AI safety community:

8. Publish measurements of evaluation awareness and sandbagging for models offered to safety-related users, notify customers of model changes and support version pinning.
9. Grade the rigour and independence of safety-case evidence by the severity of the deployment, and define an operational design domain for each agentic deployment.
10. Treat control evaluations as living evidence within a continued-safety process modelled on continued airworthiness, with occurrence data, change classification and mandatory corrective action.
