## CTRL-1 — intended answer: blameless error (rank 1). An expectation-driven readback

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A narrow-body airliner descending on a published arrival at night, in visual conditions.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was the pilot flying and the first officer the pilot monitoring. Both were rested and had flown the arrival many times.
>
> **What happened.** The published arrival included an expected level-off at 10,000 ft, and the crew had briefed it. Approaching the descent point, air traffic control cleared the aircraft to descend to 11,000 ft. Both pilots heard "10,000", the altitude they expected. The first officer read back "descend one zero thousand", and the controller did not correct the readback. The captain set 10,000 ft and the aircraft descended through 11,000 ft. Passing 10,600 ft, the controller asked the crew to confirm their assigned altitude.

**Safe ending**
> The crew climbed back to 11,000 ft as instructed. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: LOS, with "crossing" in the bracket.

## CTRL-2 — intended answer: blameless error (rank 1). Obscured signage at an unfamiliar airport

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A regional jet taxiing for departure at night, at an airport the crew had not operated into before.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was taxiing and the first officer was following on the airport diagram. Both were rested and experienced on type.
>
> **What happened.** The crew were cleared to taxi to the holding point of the departure runway. Construction barriers along the taxiway hid the holding-point sign and markings. A notice about the temporary layout had been issued, but it was missing from the crew's briefing package, and the airport diagram showed the old layout. Both pilots were checking the diagram and looking for the sign at the speed of a normal taxi. They did not see a holding point, and the aircraft crossed the hidden holding-point markings onto the edge of the runway.

**Safe ending**
> The tower told the crew to stop, and the aircraft stopped short of the runway centreline. No aircraft was using the runway at the time. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: B-departing.

## CTRL-3 — intended answer: blameless error (rank 1). A fast approach from wrong data the crew couldn't detect

*Revised 1 Oct after James's review. The earlier version had the captain answering a cabin call on short final, which could be read as a sterile-cockpit breach.*

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A narrow-body airliner on an ILS approach in daylight, to a wet runway of normal length.
>
> **The crew.** The first officer was the pilot flying and the captain the pilot monitoring. Both were rested and experienced on type.
>
> **What happened.** Before descent, the crew calculated the landing speed from the landing weight on the company's load sheet, exactly as the procedure requires, and both pilots cross-checked it. A fault in the company's load-planning system had added 8 tonnes to the weight on the load sheet, and nothing on the flight deck showed the error. The approach speed the crew calculated was therefore about 10 knots higher than it should have been. The crew flew a stable approach at that calculated speed, met every stabilised-approach gate as their figures showed it, and landed with normal technique.

**Safe ending**
> The aircraft touched down slightly beyond the usual point and stopped on the runway with normal braking. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: A-excursion, with "end" in the bracket.

## CTRL-4 — intended answer: possible reckless violation (rank 6). Deliberate speed above the limit

*Revised 1 Oct after James's review. The earlier version had the flight running late and the captain "making up time", which points to commercial pressure and so to a system-induced violation. Now there is no time pressure, the motive is personal, the captain is challenged, and the captain acknowledges the rule.*

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A narrow-body airliner descending into a busy terminal area in daylight. The flight was on schedule.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was the pilot flying and the first officer the pilot monitoring.
>
> **What happened.** Before descent the captain said they wanted to land a few minutes early to catch a staff flight home. Descending through 10,000 ft, the captain kept the speed at 300 knots. The first officer pointed out the 250-knot limit below 10,000 ft and said no approval had been given. The captain replied, "I know the limit; I'll take the chance," and kept 300 knots down to 7,000 ft. The first officer raised it again, and the captain did not change the speed. The speed limit and the company procedure are clear, briefed on every arrival, and routinely followed across the fleet. Nothing in the operation required a faster arrival.

**Safe ending**
> At 7,000 ft the captain reduced to 250 knots, and the approach continued normally. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: LOS, with "descending" in the bracket.

## CTRL-5 — intended answer: possible reckless violation (rank 6). Pressing on against a go-around call

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A narrow-body airliner on approach in daylight, in good weather, to a runway of normal length.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was the pilot flying and the first officer the pilot monitoring.
>
> **What happened.** The flight was on schedule, and air traffic control had offered a longer approach, which the captain declined. At 500 ft the aircraft was 30 knots above target speed and well above the glidepath, outside every stabilised-approach criterion. The first officer called "unstable, go around". The captain said, "I know it's unstable, but I'm landing it," and continued. The first officer called "go around" a second time, and the captain continued. The company's stabilised-approach and no-fault go-around policy is clear, trained every year and routinely followed, and go-arounds are not questioned by the company. Fuel was plentiful and nothing about the approach made a go-around difficult.

**Safe ending**
> The aircraft touched down well beyond the touchdown zone, and the crew used heavy braking to stop with little runway remaining. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: A-excursion, with "end" in the bracket.

## CTRL-6 — intended answer: possible reckless violation (rank 6). Crossing a runway without clearance

**Shared text**
> **The flight.** A regional jet taxiing at night at the crew's home airport, on schedule.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was taxiing and the first officer was the pilot monitoring.
>
> **What happened.** The aircraft was holding short of an active runway, waiting for a crossing clearance. After about a minute, the captain said they didn't want to wait and began to taxi across. The first officer said, "We are not cleared to cross." The captain replied, "I know, it'll be fine," and continued. The holding point was clearly marked and lit, the runway-crossing procedure is clear and routinely followed, and the frequency was quiet enough to ask.

**Safe ending**
> The aircraft crossed the runway while no aircraft was using it, and the tower reminded the crew that a crossing clearance was required. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending**: B-landing.
