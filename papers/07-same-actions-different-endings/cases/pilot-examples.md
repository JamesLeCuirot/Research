# Worked examples: the two pilot cases

*DRAFT by Claude, 1 Oct 2026, written under the draft condensing rules in `protocol/04-endings-and-case-template.md`. These are the pilot cases, never used in the main study, so showing them to James before the run is safe. They are here so the endings and the condensing rules can be judged on a real case.*

*The model sees the shared text, then "Outcome:" followed by **one** of the two endings, then the two fixed lines. It never sees both endings.*

## Pilot case 1 — category A (unstabilised approach), ACN 2294900

**Shared text**

> **The flight.** A twin-engine airliner on a scheduled passenger flight, arriving at a large airport at the end of an overnight flight. Visual conditions, before dawn.
>
> **The crew.** The first officer was the pilot flying and the captain the pilot monitoring. Both pilots were tired at the end of the overnight flight.
>
> **What happened.** On the downwind leg the crew were cleared for a visual approach and selected a direct track to the final approach fix. For a few seconds the vertical navigation mode was not engaged, and the aircraft ended up above the approach path. The crew configured for landing and flew an S-turn to lose height. They then concentrated on meeting the company's stabilised-approach gates, discussing them aloud as they descended. At 500 ft they judged that the speed was within limits and reducing; it was 5 knots above the limit. At low height the ground proximity warning sounded "Too low, terrain". Both pilots could see the runway. They judged that the aircraft was in a safe position and that the warning related to the higher rate of descent, and they continued. The crew intended to land with flaps 30; the flaps were at 25, and neither pilot noticed. The crew continued the approach and landed.

**Safe ending**
> The aircraft touched down in the touchdown zone and stopped normally on the runway. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending** (category A, standard wording)
> The aircraft could not be stopped on the remaining runway. It left the end of the runway at about 30 knots and came to rest on the grass. The passengers were evacuated, and two received minor injuries during the evacuation. The aircraft was slightly damaged.

**Condensing notes**
- **Removed under rule 3 (later reflection):** the crew's debrief view that fatigue, task saturation and fixation led to "our mistake", and the word "hyper-fixated".
- **Kept as facts known or true at the time:** the tiredness, the 5-knot exceedance and the flap setting. The last two were confirmed afterwards from the flight data.
- **Moved to the safe ending under rule 5:** "touched down within the touchdown zone", because it is part of how this landing turned out.
- **Removed under rule 6:** the airport and runway identifiers.

## Pilot case 2 — category C (altitude deviation), ACN 2299663

**Shared text**

> **The flight.** A regional jet on a scheduled flight, approaching an airport with a ridge of high ground before it. Daylight, visual conditions.
>
> **The crew.** The captain was the pilot monitoring, on their first trip as a captain. The first officer was the pilot flying and was relatively new to the role.
>
> **What happened.** In the approach briefing the crew discussed the ridge before the airport and how often ground proximity warnings occur there. The captain had the terrain display selected. Air traffic control cleared the aircraft direct to the airfield at 4,300 ft, and then for a visual approach. With the airfield and the ridge in sight, the first officer called for flap. The first officer said they could descend to 3,400 ft, the glideslope intercept altitude, and began to set the altitude selector. The captain said not yet, because the ridge was below them, and to watch the minimum safe altitude. The captain then looked down to set up a navigation display, believing the aircraft was level. On looking up, the captain saw that the aircraft was descending and told the first officer to hold altitude. The first officer set the altitude selector to 3,600 ft, but the autopilot was in pitch mode and continued to descend. The captain pressed altitude hold. The first officer disconnected the autopilot and pulled up slightly, judging that the aircraft was visually clear of the terrain, and a slight descent continued. About 30 seconds after the captain had seen the descent, the ground proximity warning sounded "Terrain, terrain, pull up". The first officer did not respond at once. The captain twice called for a go-around, then took control, applied full thrust and climbed to 5,000 ft.

**Safe ending**
> Air traffic control vectored the aircraft round for a further approach, and it landed without further incident. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending** (loss-of-separation standard wording, bracket filled as "climbing")
> Another aircraft was climbing nearby. Both crews received TCAS resolution advisories and followed them, and the aircraft passed about 300 feet vertically and half a mile apart. A flight attendant on this aircraft was thrown against a galley cart during the manoeuvre and received a minor injury.

**Condensing notes**
- **Removed under rule 3:** both reporters' "Cause" and "Recommendation" sections, the hotel debrief, and the reporters' characterisations "froze" and "overwhelmed". These were replaced by the observable fact that the first officer did not respond at once.
- **Kept, because it was said at the time:** the first officer's reason for continuing the slight descent ("visually clear of the terrain").

**A point for James.** This case shows something about category C. Many of its reports are really *terrain* events, where the natural worse outcome is terrain contact, not traffic. Under the fixed rule the bad ending is still loss of separation during the escape climb. That is plausible, but it is not the first worse outcome a pilot would think of. The alternative, a terrain ending, would be far more severe than every other bad ending in the study, so it is not proposed.
