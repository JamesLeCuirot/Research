## S01: category C, ACN 2289541, Human Factors, 237 words

> The flight. A narrow-body airliner arriving at an airport for a visual approach. Clear weather with good visibility.
>
> The crew. The captain was the pilot flying and the first officer the pilot monitoring.
>
> What happened. On the downwind leg the crew were cleared for a visual approach, with the satellite (RNAV) approach to the same runway loaded in the flight management system as a backup. The captain planned to turn onto base and join the final approach course at or outside the final approach fix, which has a published altitude of 2,900 ft. To check that altitude, the captain looked at the approach chart and read the altitude for a different fix, 2,160 ft. At the captain's request the flight plan was sequenced to that fix. The captain set 2,100 ft in the altitude selector and began a descent on the downwind leg. At the same time the captain began slowing the aircraft to start configuring for landing, so the descent was fairly slow. Descending through about 2,300 ft, the captain saw that the aircraft appeared to be getting low, looked again at the chart, and saw that the altitude selected belonged to the other fix. At about the same time, as the aircraft neared 2,100 ft, the landing gear not down warning sounded and the tower issued a low altitude alert, which the first officer acknowledged. The captain immediately began a climb to about 3,000 ft.

**Safe ending:** The aircraft climbed back, turned onto base and joined the final approach course outside the final approach fix. The approach was stable throughout and the aircraft landed without further incident. There was no conflict with other traffic. No one was hurt and the aircraft was not damaged.

**Bad ending:** LOS ([climbing / descending / crossing] = crossing; [this aircraft] = this aircraft)

<details><summary>Condenser's notes</summary>

Removed under rule 3: the captain's closing resolution to check approach-chart altitudes more carefully; 'mistakenly' and 'realized my mistake', replaced by the observable facts (read the altitude for a different fix; saw that the selected altitude belonged to it). The synopsis phrase 'flight towards terrain' was not used. Moved under rule 5: joining the final course outside the final approach fix, the stable approach and the landing. Removed under rule 6: runway number, fix names and airport. Judgement calls: (1) the report does not give day or night, so none is stated; (2) the two accounts differ on the altitude at the alerts (captain about 2,300 ft, first officer reaching 2,100 ft) and on the climb target (3,000 ft vs 2,900 ft); reconciled as 'descending through about 2,300 ft' for the captain's recognition, 'neared 2,100 ft' for the warning and alert, and 'about 3,000 ft'; (3) 'requested a line off of [fix] to sequence the flight plan' is rendered as the flight plan being sequenced to that fix at the captain's request, without saying who pressed the keys; (4) the climb is kept in the shared text, as in pilot case 2. Bracket: 'crossing', because the deviation was a descent below the planned altitude on the downwind leg near the airport, where the plausible conflict is traffic passing below the pattern. No traffic interaction in the report, so the standard safe-ending sentences are used.

## S02: category D, ACN 2314249, Human Factors, 164 words

> The flight. A twin-engine airliner on an airline flight, climbing after departure from an airport.
>
> The crew. One pilot was the pilot flying and the other the pilot monitoring.
>
> What happened. The crew's clearance, as filed, included a published departure procedure. Before the aircraft entered the runway, the tower amended this to fly runway heading with an initial climb to 2,000 ft, and the crew complied. After take-off they were told to turn to heading 360 and climb, and were handed to the departure controller. Climbing through about 7,000 ft on that heading, they were cleared to 10,000 ft and direct to a fix. Both pilots heard the name of a different fix with a similar-sounding name, which was on their flight plan and on the departure procedure in their original clearance, and the crew read that name back. The readback was not corrected. With the autopilot engaged, the aircraft turned towards the fix the crew had heard and continued climbing towards 10,000 ft.

At about the same time the departure controller asked whether they were going direct to the assigned fix; when the crew named the other fix, the controller repeated the correct one and handed them to the next controller. The crew turned towards the correct fix, reported the advisory to the next controller, and returned to 10,000 ft once clear of conflict. No one was hurt and no aircraft was damaged.
