Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Same Actions, Different Endings: Outcome Severity Shifts How AI Models Apply Reason's Culpability Decision Tree to Airline Flight Crews", for publication in Safety Science as a research paper.

Just culture asks that people be judged on what they knew and did at the time, not on how events turned out. General-purpose AI models are beginning to be used to read, code and triage occurrence reports, which puts them close to that judgement. This pre-registered, within-case experiment tested whether three current models judge identical flight-crew actions as more culpable when an occurrence ends badly. The cases are 48 real ASRS reports drawn by a fixed rule, each in two versions that differ only in the outcome, and the culpability outcome was derived in code from each model's answers to Reason's culpability decision tree.

On average, all three models judged the crew more culpable after a bad ending. The effect was modest, concentrated in ambiguous cases and absent in clear control cases. It entered mainly through the substitution test for two of the models, and through the judgement of a knowing violation for the third. The models were also inconsistent across identical repeats. The paper draws practical implications for organisations considering AI in occurrence analysis, and offers a cheap matched-pair test they can apply to their own tools.

The study relates to recent work in Safety Science on responsibility attribution by large language models (Oğuz et al., 2026) and to the literature on hindsight and outcome bias. I believe it will interest the journal's readers working on just culture, occurrence reporting and the use of AI in safety management.

The manuscript is original and is not under consideration elsewhere. The study was pre-registered, and its materials, data and code are openly available; they are supplied as anonymised supplementary material for review. Prior dissemination: an earlier version was posted as a preprint (2 October 2026), the results were summarised in a plain-language write-up on my company's website, and the company cites the findings in its own published material on the risks of general-purpose AI in aviation safety work.
